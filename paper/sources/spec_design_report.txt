[Heading 2] Context of the Research
This study is positioned within multilingual speech-to-text (STT) research, focusing specifically on Japanese–English code-switching in professional meeting settings. In modern Japanese workplaces, speakers frequently alternate between the two languages, often unpredictably, creating challenges for existing STT systems. Although models such as Whisper perform well on monolingual and general multilingual input, their accuracy declines sharply when processing spontaneous mixed-language speech involving different scripts and linguistic structures.
Several contextual attributes shape this research:
Mixed scripts and linguistic complexity: Japanese uses kanji, hiragana, and katakana, while English uses the Roman alphabet; current tokenisers handle these inconsistently in a single utterance.
Unpredictable code-switching: Switching is influenced by social and contextual factors and rarely follows fixed patterns.
Workplace acoustic conditions: Background noise, overlaps, hesitation and informal dialogue reduce model reliability.
Technology limitations: Existing STT systems struggle with language boundary detection and irregular alternation, leading to mistranscriptions.
The chosen topic emerged from literature identifying persistent weaknesses in multilingual ASR, especially in under-researched pairs such as Japanese–English. Prior studies on Mandarin–English code-switching (Li et al., 2022; Tseng et al., 2021) highlight problems with tokenisation, datasets and mixed-language recognition, while Japanese-focused research (中山佐保子 et al., 2021) indicates similar failings. The lack of Japanese–English code-switching datasets further underscores the need for this work.
[Heading 2] Problem Statement
Despite recent advances, multilingual STT models remain insufficient for spontaneous Japanese–English code-switching in realistic meeting environments. Key issues include:
Tokenisation weaknesses for mixed scripts within single utterances.
Limited bilingual datasets reflecting natural workplace speech and switching triggers.
Reduced robustness in noise, affecting transcription accuracy.
Poor code-switch boundary detection, leading to segmentation errors.
Given the growing use of bilingual communication in globalised Japanese companies, these shortcomings inhibit effective meeting transcription. The contextual factors above directly inform the need for a domain-specific STT solution.
[Heading 2] Dissertation Questions
The research is guided by:
How can Whisper be adapted to transcribe Japanese–English code-switching accurately in real-world conditions?
What tokenisation strategy best supports mixed Japanese and English scripts?
Does noise-augmented training improve robustness under business meeting conditions?

[Heading 1] Literature Survey:
[Heading 2] Baevski, A. et al. (2020). wav2vec 2.0: A Framework for Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning.
Baevski et al. establish how self-supervised pre-training produces robust, transferable speech representations. Although not focused on code-switching, this work underpins the rationale for fine-tuning Whisper on domain-specific data. Their results show strong noise robustness and reduced dependence on large labelled datasets, reinforcing the methodological choice to combine pre-training with targeted bilingual fine-tuning. The paper also clarifies common token alignment issues relevant to multilingual modelling.
[Heading 2] Chan, W. et al. (2015). Listen, Attend and Spell.
Chan et al. introduce LAS, an early but influential attention-based sequence-to-sequence ASR model that laid conceptual foundations for later architectures such as Whisper. The study illustrates how attention aids alignment between acoustic features and textual tokens, an issue that becomes more complex in code-switching scenarios. The authors also show the sensitivity of end-to-end models to noise, reinforcing this dissertation’s noise-augmented training strategy.
[Heading 2] Genta Indra Winata et al. (2019). Code-Switching Speech Recognition Using Joint Language Modelling.
Winata and colleagues propose unified bilingual language models to manage mixed-language utterances, showing that joint modelling improves cross-language generalisation. Their work identifies tokenisation and language identification errors as dominant failure points, directly supporting the decision to develop a Japanese–English tokeniser. Although focused on Southeast Asian languages, the structural parallels—script variation, embedded English phrases, and accent variability—offer valuable conceptual insights for this project.
[Heading 2] Li, C. et al. (2022). TALCS: An Open-Source Mandarin–English Code-Switching Corpus and ASR Baseline.
Li et al. present TALCS, a large spontaneous Mandarin–English code-switching corpus designed to represent natural bilingual speech. Their results reveal persistent ASR errors around switching points, stressing the importance of improved tokenisation and balanced bilingual data. Although the linguistic pairing differs, TALCS offers methodological guidance for building Japanese–English corpora, including transcription consistency and realistic conversational coverage. The study reinforces that bilingual ASR must be evaluated independently from monolingual performance, informing this project’s dataset design and benchmarking strategy.
[Heading 2] Mattia et al. (2019) Robust Neural Machine Translation for Clean and Noisy Speech Transcripts.
This paper investigates how neural machine translation (NMT) systems trained on clean, well-punctuated text degrade when fed noisy outputs from automatic speech recognition (ASR). The authors show that fine-tuning a strong NMT model on a mix of clean and noisy (ASR-style) transcripts significantly improves performance on both clean and noisy inputs.  
This finding is directly relevant to my Japanese–English code-switching ASR/STT project: it justifies including noisy, ASR-style and code-switched speech transcripts in your training data to improve robustness, and supports the design decision to inject realistic noise and transcription artifacts into your synthetic dataset.
[Heading 2] Radford, A. et al. (2022). Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision.
[Heading 2] Radford and colleagues introduce Whisper, a large-scale transformer ASR model trained on an extensive multilingual corpus, giving it strong generalisation across accents, domains, and noise. The paper provides essential grounding for this project by explaining Whisper’s encoder–decoder structure, log-Mel representation, and multitask capabilities. Its findings show that despite overall robustness, Whisper performs less reliably on spontaneous code-switching, highlighting a gap that motivates this research. The authors also attribute noise resilience to exposure to diverse web-sourced data, supporting this dissertation’s decision to incorporate noise-based augmentation. Whisper’s open-source implementation and scalable architecture make it a suitable backbone for fine-tuning Japanese–English code-switching models.
[Heading 2] Raj, D. et al. (2020) Frustratingly Easy Noise-aware Training of Acoustic Models.
Real-world noise, reverberation and environmental artifacts significantly degrade performance of even state-of-the-art ASR models; multi-condition training (noise augmentation, reverberation, multi-channel input) and noise-aware training consistently improve robustness. Moreover, evaluating on a broad set of noisy/reverberant datasets yields better proxies for real-world deployment than clean datasets alone.
[Heading 2] Tseng, L.-H. et al. (2021). Mandarin–English Code-Switching Speech Recognition with Self-Supervised Models.
Tseng and colleagues demonstrate that self-supervised models such as wav2vec 2.0 significantly improve bilingual ASR accuracy, particularly at switching boundaries. Their work suggests that large-scale pre-training captures cross-language acoustic regularities, a principle directly applicable to Whisper. The paper’s analyses of accent diversity, noise augmentation, and switching dynamics provide a template for evaluating fine-tuned models and support the argument that pre-training followed by targeted bilingual fine-tuning yields substantial gains.
[Heading 2] Zhou, J. et al. (2025). CS-Dialogue: A Dataset of Spontaneous Mandarin–English Code-Switching Dialogues.
Zhou et al. highlight the complexity of spontaneous bilingual speech, noting that ASR accuracy drops considerably compared with scripted datasets due to hesitations, overlaps, and rapid alternations. This work justifies the inclusion of spontaneous artefacts—fillers, hesitation markers, and informal discourse—in the synthetic Japanese–English dataset. The paper also demonstrates the necessity of realistic evaluation conditions, shaping the methodology for assessing the proposed model under naturalistic noise and switching patterns.
[Heading 2] 中山佐保子 et al. (2021) ‘単言語話者のための日英コードスイッチング音声の認識と翻訳’, 情報処理学会論文誌, 62(3), pp. 903–914.
This is one of the few studies addressing Japanese–English speech directly. 中山佐保子 and other peers  identify challenges arising from Japan’s mixed-script system and the brevity of English insertions within Japanese utterances. Their findings show that standard ASR systems fail disproportionately on mixed segments, demonstrating a clear research gap. The authors emphasise the need for improved tokenisation and morphological segmentation, providing theoretical and technical justification for the custom bilingual tokeniser used in this project.

[Heading 2] Background on the Research
This project builds on current advances in multilingual speech-to-text, focusing specifically on Japanese–English code-switching in workplace communication. While modern systems such as Whisper perform strongly in monolingual settings, existing literature shows notable weaknesses when dealing with abrupt language alternation, differing scripts, and noisy real-world meeting conditions. Limited high-quality bilingual datasets and suboptimal tokenisation further constrain performance. These gaps justify adapting Whisper through improved tokenisation, realistic synthetic datasets, and methods that enhance noise robustness.
[Heading 2] Information Used to Understand the Problem
Four areas inform problem understanding and solution design:
Multilingual ASR performance – Whisper’s architecture, benchmarks, and documented code-switching errors guide model adaptation.
Linguistic properties of Japanese–English switching – Insights into script shifts, phonetic divergence, and boundary ambiguity support the creation of a bilingual tokeniser.
Code-switching datasets and behaviour – Corpora such as TALCS and CS-Dialogue offer examples for designing synthetic scripts and annotation patterns.
Noise conditions in meetings – Research on environmental interference and acoustic artefacts shapes the augmentation strategy.
These collectively underpin the dataset design, tokeniser refinement, and evaluation plan.
[Heading 2] Research Methods
The project uses both qualitative and quantitative methods.
a. Literature Review – A structured review of multilingual ASR, tokenisation, code-switch dynamics, and noise-robust modelling provides the theoretical base.
b. Dataset Engineering – Synthetic and semi-synthetic audio will be produced using CoeiroinkTTS (Japanese), open-source English TTS, meeting-style scripts, and noise from MUSAN/DEMAND.
c. Model Development – Whisper will be adapted through a custom bilingual tokeniser, balanced bilingual corpora, and transformer fine-tuning techniques.
d. Experimental Evaluation – Performance will be assessed via WER, CER, code-switch boundary accuracy, noise-robustness tests, and qualitative usability in business scenarios.

[Heading 2] Data Structures
The system employs structured datasets to support training and evaluation:
Audio data: transcript, language-sequence labels, switch-boundary indices, and noise annotations (JSON/CSV).
Tokeniser vocabulary: token–ID mappings, reverse look-ups, and mixed-script subword rules.
Training data: Log-Mel spectrogram tensors paired with padded token-ID sequences.
Evaluation outputs: per-sample error logs, WER/CER scores, confusion matrices, and code-switch metrics.
These structures facilitate efficient processing and detailed analysis.
