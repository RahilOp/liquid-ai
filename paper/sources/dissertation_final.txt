ABSTRACT
[Title] WHISPER ADAPTATION FOR JAPANESE-ENGLISH CODE-SWITCHED SPEECH RECOGNITION
Code-switching — the practice of alternating between Japanese and English within a single utterance — poses fundamental challenges for conventional automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems trained on monolingual corpora. This dissertation addresses the development of a robust Japanese-English code-switching ASR system through parameter-efficient fine-tuning of OpenAI Whisper using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).

Three Whisper model sizes were fine-tuned: Small (244M parameters), Medium (769M), and Large-V3 (1.5B). The training corpus comprises approximately 245 bilingual speech recordings across three categories — code-switching, Japanese-only, and English-only — supplemented by noise augmentation using the MUSAN dataset at four signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels (5, 10, 15, and 20 dB), yielding 735 augmented training samples. LoRA adapters targeting the query and value projection matrices were trained using the HuggingFace PEFT library, with systematic ablation studies exploring the effect of noise augmentation level, LoRA rank, and training data composition.

The best model — Whisper Large-V3 with LoRA rank r=8, trained for 15 epochs at learning rate 1e-4 — achieves a code-switching word error rate (CS-WER) of 2.48%, a 62% relative improvement over the Whisper Medium baseline of 6.61% and a 98% reduction from the untrained Large-V3 baseline of 111.6%. All models operate significantly faster than real time (RTF < 0.15). Ablation studies demonstrate that four-level noise augmentation is essential (reducing CS-WER 20-fold compared to no augmentation), that mixed training data covering all three language types is necessary for generalisation, and that LoRA rank r=8 provides the optimal capacity-regularisation trade-off for the Large-V3 model on this small corpus.

A FastAPI web application was developed demonstrating all three fine-tuned models with side-by-side transcription from a browser interface. The findings confirm that LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large pre-trained ASR models achieves substantial improvements over monolingual baselines on code-switching speech with a small, specialised training corpus on consumer-grade GPU hardware.
This dissertation presents a four-phase iterative experimental methodology: progressively scaling from Whisper Small and Medium to Whisper Large-V3, incorporating a four-level noise augmentation strategy using the MUSAN corpus, conducting controlled ablation studies across LoRA rank, noise level, and dataset composition, and extending training to fifteen epochs to maximise convergence. Evaluation employs word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) computed separately for code-switching, Japanese-only, and English-only audio segments, alongside real-time factor (RTF) measurement to assess practical deployability. These results derive from a small held-out partition (approximately 105 utterances) drawn from the same two Azure neural voices used in training, and should be read as promising evidence from a controlled study rather than as a measure of speaker-independent or natural-speech performance.

[Heading 1] Introduction
This chapter introduces the research problem addressed in this dissertation — automatic speech recognition for Japanese-English code-switching — and establishes the motivation, aims, and objectives of the project. It outlines the overall approach adopted, summarises the outcomes achieved, and describes the organisation of the remaining chapters.
[Heading 2] Chapter Introduction
Code-switching is the practice of alternating between two or more languages within a single conversation or utterance. In the Japanese-English bilingual context, a speaker might begin a sentence in Japanese and complete it in English, or embed English technical terms within an otherwise Japanese utterance. This linguistic behaviour is pervasive in professional and academic settings in Japan, yet poses serious challenges for commercial ASR systems designed for monolingual input. This section summarises the purpose of Chapter 1 and its structure.
In the Japanese-English context, this phenomenon is pervasive in technology companies, academic institutions, and international business environments across Japan, where English technical vocabulary is routinely embedded within otherwise Japanese discourse. Speakers may interject English product names, programming terms, or business jargon — such as 'update', 'meeting', or 'deadline' — without interrupting the Japanese syntactic flow. This practise, sometimes termed Wasei-eigo (和製英語, Japanese-made English), represents a stable and sociolinguistically motivated form of bilingual speech rather than a transient performance error (Poplack, 1980). The computational challenge it poses — recognising abrupt phonological and orthographic transitions within a single utterance — has received comparatively little systematic attention in the ASR literature relative to the Spanish-English and Mandarin-English language pairs.
[Heading 2] Problem Statement
Contemporary ASR systems — including leading commercial products — are trained predominantly on monolingual corpora. When presented with code-switching speech, these systems fail in several characteristic ways: they may collapse all output to a single language, produce garbled mixed output, or enter repetition loops. The fundamental cause is a distributional mismatch between monolingual training data and bilingual test conditions.
The Japanese-English language pair is among the most challenging for code-switching ASR. Japanese and English differ in virtually every phonological, morphological, and syntactic dimension: Japanese has predominantly open syllable structure (consonant-vowel) while English allows complex consonant clusters; Japanese is largely subject-object-verb while English is subject-verb-object; Japanese uses three distinct writing systems (hiragana, katakana, kanji) while English uses the Latin alphabet. At the acoustic level, the vowel formant spaces of the two languages overlap only partially, requiring a model to master two very different phonological systems simultaneously and detect abrupt transitions between them in real-time speech.
Building a dedicated system requires: (a) a sufficiently rich multilingual corpus; (b) a model architecture capable of joint modelling of both languages; (c) a training strategy that does not require billions of parameters to be re-trained from scratch; and (d) a noise-robust augmentation strategy to compensate for the small size of a specialised bilingual corpus. This dissertation addresses all four requirements through an iterative experimental programme spanning four distinct phases.
The commercial consequences of this limitation are tangible. Voice-enabled products deployed in Japan — including customer service chatbots, meeting transcription tools, and voice search interfaces — encounter code-switching speech routinely in professional contexts, yet are typically built on monolingual English or Japanese engines with no code-switching capability. A bilingual employee asking a meeting transcription system to capture a sentence such as '次のmeetingはいつ？' (When is the next meeting?) will receive either a garbled output or a complete language collapse, degrading trust in the technology and reducing adoption rates. The economic value of an accurate code-switching ASR system in this context extends beyond transcription accuracy: it enables downstream natural language processing pipelines — such as action item extraction, speaker attribution, and meeting summarisation — to operate on correctly transcribed bilingual text rather than corrupted monolingual output.
[Heading 2] Project Aims and Objectives
The overarching aim of this project is to develop a practical Japanese-English code-switching ASR system that significantly outperforms untrained baselines while remaining deployable on consumer-grade GPU hardware with a small, specialised training corpus. The specific objectives are: (1) to assemble and preprocess a bilingual speech corpus with aligned transcriptions; (2) to implement a noise augmentation pipeline using the MUSAN corpus at multiple SNR levels; (3) to apply LoRA-based fine-tuning to Whisper Small, Medium, and Large-V3, exploring LoRA rank, learning rate, and target attention modules; (4) to conduct controlled ablation studies quantifying the individual contribution of noise augmentation, dataset composition, and LoRA rank to model performance; (5) to evaluate all models using WER, CER, and RTF on a held-out test set with a fixed random seed; and (6) to build a deployable web application demonstrating all three fine-tuned models.
These aims are operationalised through three explicit research questions that structure the experimental design and direct the interpretation of results:
RQ1: To what extent can LoRA fine-tuning of Whisper reduce code-switching WER for Japanese-English speech below the performance of standard monolingual ASR baselines, and what absolute WER is achievable with a corpus of approximately 245 utterances?
RQ2: Which LoRA hyperparameters — rank, target projection modules, and learning rate — most significantly influence code-switching recognition accuracy, and how do these interact with the noise augmentation strategy?
RQ3: What is the inference efficiency profile of LoRA-adapted Whisper across model scales (Small, Medium, Large-V3), and does parameter-efficient fine-tuning preserve the real-time processing capability required for practical deployment on consumer-grade hardware?
RQ1 is addressed in Chapter 5 (Sections 5.2.1 and 5.2.3); RQ2 is addressed through the ablation studies in Sections 5.2.5–5.2.7; RQ3 is addressed in Section 5.2.8. Together, these questions constitute a coherent empirical programme that connects the literature review (Chapter 2) to the experimental results and conclusions (Chapters 5 and 6).
[Heading 2] Approach
The project adopts an iterative experimental approach informed by the literature on parameter-efficient fine-tuning and multilingual speech recognition. OpenAI Whisper was selected as the base model for its strong multilingual pre-training across 96 languages (including Japanese), open-source availability, and demonstrated robustness to noise and accent variation. LoRA was chosen as the fine-tuning strategy because it reduces trainable parameters from 1.5 billion to approximately 3.9 million — less than 0.3% of the Large-V3 base model — while achieving performance competitive with full fine-tuning. Training data was collected from bilingual speakers and augmented with MUSAN noise at four SNR levels (20, 15, 10, 5 dB). Experiments proceeded through four phases, each building on lessons from the previous. The emphasis of the work shifted during the project relative to the original proposal: the proposal placed considerable weight on developing a custom bilingual tokeniser, but a pilot evaluation showed that Whisper's existing multilingual sub-word tokeniser already covers Japanese and English adequately for code-switching transcription. Effort was therefore redirected to LoRA adaptation, noise augmentation, and the deployment artefact — areas where the gap in the published literature for low-resource Japanese-English code-switching was sharper.
[Heading 2] Outcome
The primary outcome is a LoRA-adapted Whisper Large-V3 model achieving 2.48% CS-WER, 0.83% JA-WER, and 2.22% EN-WER on the held-out test set — a 62% relative improvement over the Whisper Medium baseline of 6.61% CS-WER and a 98% reduction from the untrained Large-V3 baseline of 111.6%. All three models operate far faster than real time (RTF < 0.15). Systematic ablation studies provide quantified guidance on the contribution of each design decision. A FastAPI web application demonstrates all three models in a browser-based interface accepting WAV, MP3, M4A, and FLAC audio.
From an engineering perspective, the project validates a complete research-to-deployment pipeline: corpus collection and annotation, data augmentation, iterative LoRA fine-tuning, controlled ablation, quantitative evaluation, and production web application. Each step produced durable artefacts — annotated transcription files, augmented audio, trained adapter checkpoints, evaluation JSON archives, and a containerised application — that collectively constitute a reproducible research package. The modular design of the training pipeline, in which model size, LoRA rank, target modules, learning rate, and training duration are all configurable parameters, means that the codebase can be straightforwardly extended to new language pairs, new Whisper model versions, or different PEFT methods without architectural changes.

[Heading 2] Research Background
Automatic speech recognition has undergone fundamental transformation since the advent of deep learning. The dominant prior paradigm — Hidden Markov Model combined with Gaussian Mixture Model acoustic models (HMM-GMM) — was superseded by deep neural network acoustic models (DNN-HMM) and subsequently by end-to-end architectures. Connectionist Temporal Classification (Graves et al., 2006) enabled direct sequence-to-sequence training without explicit phoneme alignment. Attention-based encoder-decoder models (Chan et al., 2015) eliminated the need for language model fusion and enabled training entirely from audio-transcription pairs.
The transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017), originally proposed for machine translation, proved highly effective for ASR by enabling the model to directly relate any position in the input sequence to any other, overcoming the vanishing gradient limitation of recurrent networks. Multi-head self-attention computes Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k))V in parallel across multiple representation subspaces, capturing both local and long-range acoustic dependencies.
OpenAI Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) represents a landmark advance in large-scale multilingual ASR. Trained on 680,000 hours of weakly-supervised web-scraped audio across 96 languages including Japanese, Whisper uses a multitask training objective encompassing transcription, translation, language identification, and voice activity detection. Its encoder processes log-mel spectrograms (80-channel for Small/Medium, 128-channel for Large-V3) through convolutional downsampling layers and transformer blocks. The decoder generates text tokens autoregressively using cross-attention over the encoder output. Critically, Whisper supports forced_decoder_ids — a mechanism for priming the decoder with a target language token — enabling targeted bilingual transcription without additional language identification components.
The Whisper Large-V3 architecture comprises a 32-layer convolutional-transformer encoder and a 32-layer transformer decoder, with attention dimension d_model = 1,280 and 16 attention heads per layer. The encoder processes the 128-channel log-mel spectrogram through two convolutional downsampling layers (each with stride 2) before the transformer stack, reducing the time resolution by a factor of four and producing frame-level representations at 10-frame intervals (100 ms). The decoder employs masked self-attention and cross-attention over the encoder output, with a vocabulary of 51,865 tokens covering characters, subwords, and special task tokens for all 96 supported languages. The total parameter count of 1.55 billion includes 304 million in the encoder, 704 million in the decoder, and 547 million in the shared input and output embedding matrix. For fine-tuning, the embedding matrices and all layer normalisation parameters are frozen alongside the base attention and feed-forward weights; only the LoRA adapter matrices B and A — attached to the q_proj and v_proj of every decoder attention layer — receive gradient updates. The encoder is frozen entirely, on the basis that the phonological representations learnt during Whisper's pre-training across 96 languages are already well-suited to the acoustic characteristics of Japanese and English speech, and that fine-tuning for code-switching requires principally adjusting the decoder's language model prior rather than the acoustic front-end (Hsu et al. 2023).
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has emerged as a critical methodology for adapting large pre-trained models to new tasks without the prohibitive cost of full fine-tuning. Among PEFT methods, LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) achieves the widest adoption due to its simplicity and effectiveness. Prefix tuning (Li and Liang, 2021), prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021), and adapter modules (Houlsby et al., 2019) are alternative approaches; however, LoRA adapters can be merged with base model weights at inference time, incurring zero additional latency over the base model. Code-switching in speech has been studied linguistically for decades (Poplack, 1980; Myers-Scotton, 1993), but its treatment in ASR systems has received sustained attention only in the past decade as deep learning made it feasible to build models for complex multilingual distributions.
The evolution of automatic speech recognition from classical statistical methods to modern deep learning architectures has been one of the most consequential developments in applied machine learning. The Hidden Markov Model–Gaussian Mixture Model (HMM-GMM) paradigm, foundational to commercial ASR systems from the 1980s onwards (Rabiner, 1989), decomposed the recognition problem into an acoustic model estimating phoneme emission probabilities and a language model capturing word sequence likelihoods. Whilst HMM-GMM systems achieved competitive performance under matched acoustic conditions, their rigid assumptions of Gaussian-distributed acoustic features and first-order Markovian temporal dependencies limited robustness to environmental variability and unseen speaker characteristics. The transition to discriminative deep neural network acoustic models (Hinton et al., 2012) delivered substantial word error rate reductions across standard benchmarks by replacing the Gaussian mixture with deep feedforward networks, enabling richer acoustic representations without discarding the principled temporal alignment machinery of the HMM framework.
End-to-end architectures subsequently eliminated the dependency on expert-designed phoneme lexicons and pronunciation dictionaries. The Listen, Attend and Spell model (Chan et al., 2015) demonstrated that an attention-based encoder-decoder trained on mel-filterbank features could directly map acoustic input to character sequences, whilst Connectionist Temporal Classification (Graves et al., 2006) provided an alternative formulation by marginalising over all valid token-to-frame alignments during training without requiring explicit segmentation. The Conformer architecture (Gulati et al., 2020) subsequently demonstrated that combining multi-head self-attention with convolutional layers — enabling simultaneous modelling of global context and local acoustic detail within each encoder block — yielded state-of-the-art results on LibriSpeech and multiple multilingual benchmarks, establishing the architectural basis upon which the Whisper encoder was designed.
Japanese automatic speech recognition presents challenges qualitatively distinct from those encountered in European languages, arising from the intersection of linguistic, phonological, and orthographic properties. At the phonological level, Japanese employs mora-timed rhythm (in contrast to English stress-timed rhythm), with prosodic boundaries signalled by pitch accent rather than lexical stress. This stands in sharp contrast to English, which permits complex onset and coda clusters and employs vowel reduction in unstressed syllables — a distinction with direct acoustic consequences for bilingual speakers transitioning between languages mid-utterance. At the orthographic level, Japanese employs three co-occurring writing systems: hiragana (phonetic syllabary for native vocabulary and grammatical inflections), katakana (phonetic syllabary for loanwords and emphasis), and kanji (morphographic characters derived from Chinese, numbering approximately 2,136 in everyday use). A typical Japanese sentence freely intermixes all three scripts, requiring the ASR output layer to assign the correct script to each morpheme based on lexical identity rather than phonological form — a task with no direct European-language analogue.
The emergence of large-scale multilingual ASR models trained across dozens of languages simultaneously has transformed the landscape of cross-lingual speech technology. The massively multilingual speech model of Pratap et al. (2020) demonstrated that a single model trained on 51 languages could outperform independently trained monolingual models on low-resource languages through beneficial cross-lingual transfer. Google's Universal Speech Model (Zhang et al., 2023) extended this paradigm to over 300 languages, establishing that scale in both model parameters and training data diversity yields qualitative improvements in robustness to accent variation, noise, and domain shift. OpenAI Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) occupies a distinct position in this landscape by virtue of its training corpus composition: 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio, including 117,000 hours of non-English data, distinguished by the use of audio paired with human-generated rather than machine-translated transcriptions wherever available. This training methodology, combined with a multitask objective encompassing transcription, translation, voice activity detection, and language identification, produced a model with exceptional generalisation to unseen speakers and acoustic conditions — precisely the properties most valuable for a specialised code-switching application with a small training corpus.
The specific challenge of code-switching ASR has attracted growing research attention since approximately 2015, driven by the rapid uptake of voice interfaces in multilingual markets including South-East Asia, India, and East Asia. Early work by Shan et al. (2019) and Indra et al. (2020) demonstrated that dedicated architectural changes — such as language-aware attention mechanisms and meta-learning initialisation — could improve code-switching performance on Mandarin-English corpora. However, these approaches required either large labelled code-switching corpora or computationally expensive meta-training procedures, limiting their practical utility for low-resource language pairs. The advent of large-scale multilingual pre-training fundamentally altered this calculus: by providing a strong acoustic prior across dozens of languages, models such as Whisper reduce the in-domain data requirement for code-switching adaptation from thousands of hours to hundreds of utterances, as demonstrated by Peng et al. (2023) on Mandarin-English and by this dissertation on the Japanese-English pair.
The linguistic underpinning of code-switching behaviour is formalised by the Matrix Language Frame (MLF) model (Myers-Scotton, 1993). The MLF model proposes that in bilingual code-switching utterances, one language — designated the Matrix Language — provides the morphosyntactic frame, supplying grammatical and functional morphemes, whilst the other — the Embedded Language — contributes content morphemes at positions permitted by the matrix grammar. In Japanese-English discourse, Japanese consistently functions as the matrix language: Japanese particles, verb endings, and topic markers provide the clausal scaffold, whilst English content words — predominantly nouns and technical terms — are inserted at positions where Japanese syntax permits nominal phrases or complement clauses. This structural asymmetry has direct consequences for ASR: English lexical items occur in acoustic contexts shaped by Japanese prosody and co-articulation, creating distributions that a monolingual English model has not observed. The MLF model further distinguishes intrasentential switching — mixing within a single clause — from intersentential switching between clauses, with the former imposing greater demands on an ASR decoder because the language of individual tokens must be determined from local acoustic and phonotactic context alone. The corpus employed in this dissertation includes utterances exhibiting both types, providing a representative sample of the structural variation that a deployed bilingual ASR system would encounter.
[Heading 2] Literature Review
The most directly relevant prior work falls into three clusters: multilingual Whisper fine-tuning, code-switching ASR, and LoRA for speech models.
Multilingual Whisper Fine-Tuning. Peng et al. (2023) extended the LORA training of ASR Models to code-switching on Mandarin-English corpora, achieving competitive performance against models trained from scratch on much larger datasets. Their work confirmed that Whisper's multilingual pre-training provides a strong prior that substantially reduces in-domain data requirements for code-switching adaptation — a finding directly motivating the present study.
The fine-tuning approach adopted by Peng et al. (2023) differs from that employed in this dissertation in one important respect: rather than LoRA, they applied full fine-tuning of all Whisper parameters using a relatively large labelled corpus. Hsu et al. (2023) subsequently demonstrated that LoRA applied to Whisper achieves comparable or superior WER to full fine-tuning on small datasets — a finding directly motivating the choice of LoRA in this project — because full fine-tuning on a small dataset tends to overfit to speaker-specific phonological patterns whilst forgetting Whisper's general multilingual prior. This distinction is particularly critical for the present project, where the training set contains approximately 245 unique utterances: a full fine-tuning of 1.5 billion parameters on 245 samples would almost certainly overfit catastrophically, whilst LoRA's 3.9 million trainable parameters represent a far more conservative adaptation capacity appropriate to the dataset size.
Code-Switching ASR. Shan et al. (2019) proposed a language-aware attention mechanism that explicitly models language boundaries within attention weights, achieving improvements on Mandarin-English benchmarks. Indra et al. (2020) explored meta-learning for code-switching, finding that few-shot adaptation from pre-trained models outperforms training from scratch — consistent with the approach adopted in this project.
LoRA for Speech Models. Hsu et al. (2023) applied LoRA to Whisper for ASR and found that fine-tuning only the attention projection matrices — specifically query (q_proj) and value (v_proj) — yields the best performance-efficiency trade-off, and that lower ranks (r=8) outperform higher ranks (r=64) on small datasets. A key gap identified across this literature is the absence of controlled ablation studies on noise augmentation strategies for code-switching ASR — a gap this dissertation directly addresses through a four-level augmentation comparison.
Evaluation Metrics for Code-Switching ASR. The selection of appropriate evaluation metrics for mixed-language systems requires careful consideration beyond standard word error rate. Yilmaz et al. (2018) argued that WER computation applied to mixed-language sequences is sensitive to normalisation choices, particularly regarding how language-mixing tokens are tokenised and whether language-specific spelling conventions are enforced. Character Error Rate (CER) provides a complementary perspective for languages such as Japanese, where word boundary detection is itself non-trivial; a system may correctly recognise all kanji characters whilst incorrectly segmenting them into word units, yielding misleading WER values whilst CER remains low. The mixed-language CER employed in this dissertation therefore provides a more granular diagnostic of recognition quality, particularly for Japanese-script segments where a single kanji substitution carries the same WER penalty as a full English word substitution. Watanabe et al. (2018) noted in the development of the ESPnet toolkit that evaluation pipelines for multilingual ASR systems benefit from language-stratified metrics that separately quantify within-language and cross-language recognition accuracy, motivating the separate reporting of CS-WER, JA-WER, and EN-WER adopted in this project.
Data Augmentation for Low-Resource ASR. Given the small corpus employed in this dissertation (approximately 245 utterances), data augmentation is not merely beneficial but essential. Park et al. (2019) introduced SpecAugment, a technique for augmenting log-mel spectrograms by masking contiguous blocks of time steps and frequency bins directly, demonstrating substantial WER improvements even without additional training data. Yilmaz et al. (2018) investigated acoustic and textual data augmentation specifically for code-switching ASR, finding that noise augmentation at multiple SNR levels outperformed SpecAugment alone for code-switching tasks, because the acoustic diversity introduced by environmental noise more faithfully approximates the recording conditions of naturalistic bilingual speech collected in non-studio environments. Ko et al. (2015) demonstrated speed perturbation as an effective complementary augmentation strategy for large vocabulary ASR, creating pitch-normalised variants at 0.9× and 1.1× speed to simulate natural speaker rate variation. Whilst speed perturbation and room impulse response convolution were not implemented in the present work due to computational constraints, they represent well-validated directions for future investigation, as discussed in Chapter 6.
[Heading 2] Theory
The Transformer Architecture. Each transformer encoder block contains a multi-head self-attention (MHSA) sub-layer and a position-wise feed-forward (FFN) sub-layer, both followed by layer normalisation and residual connections. The MHSA sub-layer computes attention scores as Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k))V, where Q=XW_Q, K=XW_K, V=XW_V are the query, key, and value projections, and d_k is the key dimension. Multi-head attention applies this h times in parallel with distinct learned projections then concatenates and projects the results. Whisper Small has 12 encoder and 12 decoder layers; Medium has 24; Large-V3 has 32, with attention dimension 1280.
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) exploits the empirical observation that weight updates during fine-tuning lie in a low-dimensional subspace. For a weight matrix W of shape (m x n), LoRA represents the update as delta_W = B * A, where B is (m x r) and A is (r x n), with rank r << min(m,n). During training, W is frozen and only A and B are updated. At inference, the adapted weight is W' = W + (lora_alpha / r) * B * A. For Whisper Large-V3 with r=8 on the q_proj and v_proj matrices (dimension 1280x1280), LoRA introduces approximately 3.9 million trainable parameters — 0.26% of the 1.5 billion base model parameters. This makes training feasible on a single consumer-grade GPU. Adapter B is initialised to zero (so delta_W = 0 at training start) and A is initialised with a random Gaussian. This ensures that at the start of fine-tuning the model outputs are identical to the base model.
The mathematical relationship between LoRA rank and model capacity merits further examination. For Whisper Large-V3, each attention layer contains four projection matrices: q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, and out_proj, each of dimension 1,280 × 1,280. Full fine-tuning of all four matrices across all 32 decoder layers yields 32 × 4 × 1,280² ≈ 210 million trainable parameters in the decoder alone, before considering encoder and embedding parameters. LoRA with r=8 on q_proj and v_proj reduces this to 32 × 2 × 2 × 1,280 × 8 ≈ 1.3 million parameters — a 161-fold reduction. This parameter efficiency has a direct practical implication: the Adam optimiser (Kingma and Ba, 2014), which maintains first and second moment estimates for each trainable parameter, requires 2 × 1.3M × 4 bytes ≈ 10 MB of additional GPU memory for optimiser states, compared to approximately 1.7 GB for full fine-tuning of these layers. This reduction, combined with the frozen base model requiring no gradient computation, is the primary reason that Large-V3 LoRA training fits comfortably within the 16 GB VRAM budget of a Google Colab T4 GPU whilst full fine-tuning would require model parallelism across multiple GPUs.
The initialisation strategy for LoRA matrices — B initialised to zero, A to a random Gaussian — deserves particular attention in the code-switching context. Because B=0 at initialisation, the product BA = 0, meaning that the adapted weight W' = W + (α/r)·BA equals the frozen base weight W at the start of fine-tuning. This 'identity initialisation' of the adapter ensures that the model begins fine-tuning from the full strength of Whisper's multilingual pre-training, without any disruptive random perturbation of the pretrained representations. For code-switching ASR, this is particularly valuable: the base Whisper Large-V3 model already has high-quality phonological representations for both Japanese and English individually; fine-tuning needs only to teach the model to handle language transitions and to prioritise Japanese-script output — a relatively small adaptation task that LoRA's conservative initialisation strategy is well-suited to address (Hu et al., 2021). The overall architecture, with LoRA adapters inserted into the encoder and decoder attention layers, is illustrated in Figure 1.

Noise Augmentation. The MUSAN corpus (Snyder et al., 2015) provides music, speech, and ambient noise recordings widely used for data augmentation in speech processing. Noise augmentation at a target SNR level is implemented as: y_aug = y_clean + alpha * y_noise, where alpha is chosen such that 10*log10(||y_clean||^2 / (alpha^2 * ||y_noise||^2)) equals the target SNR in decibels. Applying augmentation at four SNR levels (20, 15, 10, 5 dB) increases the effective training set by a factor of five and trains the model to be robust across a range of acoustic conditions — from lightly noisy (SNR 20 dB) to severely degraded (SNR 5 dB).
Spectral Feature Extraction. The front-end of Whisper processes raw waveforms through a log-mel filterbank feature extractor. A short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is first applied with a 25 ms Hann window and 10 ms frame shift, producing a complex spectrogram. The magnitude spectrogram is then projected onto a bank of triangular mel-scale filters, producing an 80-dimensional (Small/Medium) or 128-dimensional (Large-V3) feature vector per frame. The resulting log-mel spectrogram is normalised to the range [−1, 1] via global mean-variance normalisation estimated over the training corpus. The 128-channel configuration of Large-V3 captures higher-frequency acoustic detail than the 80-channel configuration of smaller models, which is particularly relevant for Japanese, where pitch accent patterns and fricative consonants (e.g., /s/, /ʃ/) carry phonemic distinctions in high-frequency bands. This architectural difference necessitates separate processor instances for Large-V3 versus Small/Medium, as the feature dimensions are incompatible.
Training Objective. Whisper and the LoRA-adapted variants are trained using teacher-forced cross-entropy loss over the decoder token sequence. For a reference token sequence y = (y_1, ..., y_T) and model parameters θ, the loss is: L(θ) = −(1/T) Σ_{t=1}^{T} log P(y_t | y_{<t}, x; θ), where x denotes the encoder output derived from the log-mel spectrogram, and P is computed by the decoder's softmax output layer. Padding positions (set to −100 in the label tensor) are masked from the sum, ensuring that the loss gradient is computed only over meaningful token positions. The LoRA update means that only the matrices A and B in the adapter modules receive gradients, whilst all other parameters contribute to the forward pass but receive no gradient update. The effective parameter count subject to optimisation is therefore 3.9 million for Large-V3 (r=8, q+v projections across 32 layers), compared to 1.5 billion for full fine-tuning — reducing GPU memory for optimiser states by over 99%.
[Heading 2] Terminology
Word Error Rate (WER): proportion of incorrectly recognised words, computed as (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference_word_count. Values above 100% are possible when insertions outnumber reference words. Character Error Rate (CER): analogous to WER at the character level; particularly relevant for Japanese where word boundaries are not marked orthographically. Code-Switching (CS): alternating between two or more languages within a conversation or utterance. LoRA Rank (r): inner dimension of LoRA rank-decomposition matrices; smaller r gives more regularisation, larger r gives more capacity. Real-Time Factor (RTF): ratio of processing time to audio duration; RTF < 1.0 means faster than real time. PEFT: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning. MUSAN: a publicly available Music, Speech, and Noise corpus used for acoustic augmentation. Forced Decoder IDs: a Whisper mechanism that prepends specific tokens (e.g., language and task tokens) to every decoder sequence, guiding output language selection.

[Heading 2] Project Specification

The non-functional requirements reflect constraints imposed by the deployment environment and academic reproducibility standards. NFR1 (VRAM < 24 GB for inference) was designed to accommodate both the development hardware (Google Colab T4, 16 GB) and common consumer GPUs such as the NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB), ensuring that the trained models are deployable beyond the specific hardware used for training. NFR2 (training completable within one week) was satisfied for all phases: Whisper Small and Medium each trained in under 2 hours for 3 epochs; Large-V3 Phase 4 (15 epochs) completed in approximately 18 hours on the target hardware. [Heading 2] Research Methods
The project employs a quantitative experimental methodology. The primary research question — 'Can LoRA fine-tuning of Whisper achieve competitive Japanese-English code-switching ASR with a small bilingual corpus?' — is addressed through controlled ablation experiments in which one independent variable is changed at a time while all others are held constant. This follows the tradition of systematic ablation studies in machine learning research (Meyes et al., 2019).
Justification of Evaluation Metrics. The primary evaluation metric, CS-WER, was selected over alternatives including token error rate and mixed-language BLEU (bilingual evaluation understudy) for three reasons. First, WER has the longest history of adoption in speech recognition research, enabling direct comparison with published results. Second, the jiwer library implements WER computation consistent with the NIST scoring convention, ensuring reproducibility. Third, WER is directly interpretable in terms of word-level accuracy, aligning with the end-user requirement of producing readable transcriptions. The CS-WER denominator is the total number of reference words across all code-switching test utterances, computed collectively rather than per-utterance, to avoid numerical instability from very short utterances where a single error could produce WER values exceeding 100%. CER complements WER for Japanese-script evaluation: since morpheme boundaries are not delimited by whitespace in Japanese, word segmentation tools such as MeCab apply linguistic rules that do not generalise to code-switching output. Computing CER at the Unicode character level — treating each hiragana, katakana, kanji, and Latin character as an atomic unit — avoids this segmentation dependency entirely. Real-Time Factor was measured using Python's time.perf_counter() across the complete inference pipeline, including feature extraction and greedy decoding with forced language priming, but excluding model loading time. Five warm-up inferences preceded timing on each model to exclude CUDA kernel compilation overhead from the measurements.
The primary evaluation metric is CS-WER, computed using the JiWER library with default tokenisation applied to mixed-language reference-hypothesis pairs. Secondary metrics include JA-WER, EN-WER, CER for each language category, and RTF measured on the target hardware. The train/test split is 70%/30% with a fixed random seed (42) applied identically across all experiments to ensure valid cross-experiment comparisons. The experimental programme proceeded through four phases: Phase 1 — Small and Medium baselines; Phase 2 — Large-V3 initial attempt (data bug, discarded); Phase 3 — Large-V3 with bug fix, three configurations; Phase 4 — extended training of the best Phase 3 configuration to 15 epochs. Ablation studies were conducted using Whisper Small (fastest training), varying noise augmentation level, LoRA rank, and training dataset composition one factor at a time.
The choice of Whisper Small as the ablation vehicle — rather than Large-V3 — was dictated by computational efficiency: each ablation configuration required a complete 3-epoch training run, and conducting all twelve ablation variants (4 noise levels × 4 ranks × 4 dataset compositions) on Large-V3 would have required approximately 216 GPU-hours, compared to approximately 24 GPU-hours for Small. The assumption underlying this choice — that the relative ranking of configurations on Small is predictive of their ranking on Large-V3 — is supported by the finding of Hsu et al. (2023) that LoRA rank and module selection exhibit similar sensitivity patterns across model sizes. The one exception identified in this project is the optimal rank: r=16 is optimal for Small, whilst r=8 is optimal for Large-V3, consistent with the well-established empirical finding that larger models require less adapter capacity per unit of performance gain (Hu et al., 2021). This cross-scale discrepancy should be borne in mind when interpreting ablation results in Chapter 5.
[Heading 2] Design Considerations
Data Pipeline Design. Audio files are loaded at 16 kHz using librosa. For Whisper Small and Medium, the WhisperProcessor extracts an 80-channel log-mel spectrogram with 25 ms windows and 10 ms hop, producing fixed-length (80 x 3000) tensors representing 30 seconds. For Large-V3, 128-channel spectrograms are produced. Audio shorter than 30 seconds is zero-padded; longer audio is truncated (not an issue — all corpus utterances are under 15 seconds). Transcription labels are read from the full_transcription column of Excel spreadsheets (a critical detail: the incorrect column name transcription produces empty strings and invalid WER, as discovered in Phase 2). Labels are tokenised, padded to batch maximum length, and padding positions masked with -100 to exclude them from the cross-entropy loss. The leading BOS token is stripped from the label tensor, as the Seq2SeqTrainer prepends it during the forward pass.
LoRA Configuration. Based on Hsu et al. (2023), the initial configuration targets the query projection (q_proj) and value projection (v_proj) matrices across all transformer layers. Alternative configurations in Phase 3 add the key projection (k_proj) and output projection (out_proj). LoRA dropout is set to 0.1 throughout. The scaling factor lora_alpha equals twice the rank (e.g., alpha=16 for r=8), following the convention in Hu et al. (2021). This choice gives the effective update scale (lora_alpha/r) = 2, providing a reasonable learning signal amplitude without requiring separate hyperparameter search for lora_alpha.
Training Strategy. Training uses the HuggingFace Seq2SeqTrainer with bfloat16 (bf16) mixed precision for Large-V3 and float16 for Small/Medium. Effective batch size is 16 (4 per device, 4 gradient accumulation steps). Each outer loop 'epoch' corresponds to one pass over the full training set, followed immediately by evaluation on all three test subsets. Warmup steps (5) are applied in the first epoch only. No learning rate schedule is used — a constant learning rate throughout simplifies the hyperparameter space and is appropriate given the small dataset and moderate epoch count. LoRA adapters are saved (not the merged model) at each best CS-WER checkpoint, reducing checkpoint storage from ~6 GB (merged Large-V3) to 7-60 MB (adapter only).
The decision to save adapter-only checkpoints (7–60 MB) rather than merged model checkpoints (~6 GB) has several practical advantages beyond storage efficiency. First, it allows the base Whisper model to be updated independently of the adapter: should OpenAI release an improved Whisper Large-V3 variant, the LoRA adapter can be re-applied without retraining, provided the model architecture (particularly the attention dimension and layer count) is unchanged. Second, multiple domain-specific adapters can coexist alongside a single shared base model, enabling a 'multi-tenant' deployment in which different adapter variants — for example, one fine-tuned on medical Japanese-English and another on legal Japanese-English — can be switched at inference time with negligible overhead. Third, the small checkpoint size facilitates version control: all four LoRA adapter variants produced in this project (Small, Medium, Large-V3 Phase 3, Large-V3 Phase 4) are tracked in Git alongside the training scripts, evaluation results, and transcription spreadsheets, providing a complete audit trail of the experimental programme that would not be feasible with 6 GB full-model checkpoints.
Forced Decoder IDs and Inference. A critical design decision is enabling forced_decoder_ids during inference. By supplying the processor's Japanese language token and 'transcribe' task token as forced prefix tokens, the decoder is guided to produce Japanese (and mixed Japanese-English) output. Without this forcing, the untrained Large-V3 produces predominantly English output for code-switching audio, because English text dominates Whisper's pre-training data. The forcing is disabled during training (model.config.forced_decoder_ids = None) and re-enabled during evaluation.
Web Application Architecture. The web application is built using FastAPI. All three LoRA models are loaded at startup and held in GPU memory for zero-latency inference. The UI is served as a single-page HTML/JS application that communicates with the FastAPI backend via multipart form upload.
The selection of LoRA over full fine-tuning was determined by hardware constraints. Full fine-tuning of Whisper Large-V3 (1.5 billion parameters) in float32 would require approximately 24 GB of GPU memory for model weights alone, plus an equivalent amount for gradients and Adam optimiser states — a combined total exceeding the 16 GB VRAM of the available hardware when accounting for batch activations. LoRA reduces trainable parameters from 1.5 billion to 3.9 million, reducing gradient and optimiser state memory by over 99%, and enabling training with an effective batch size of 16 within 6 GB of VRAM. An alternative PEFT method, adapter modules (Houlsby et al., 2019), inserts trainable bottleneck layers between transformer sub-layers, incurring additional inference latency proportional to the number of adapter layers. LoRA avoids this inference overhead because adapted weights can be merged with base weights post-training — yielding zero additional latency over the base model — and was therefore preferred for the production deployment scenario represented by the FastAPI web application.
Hyperparameter Selection Rationale. The initial LoRA rank r=8 was selected based on the recommendation of Hsu et al. (2023), who found that r=8 applied to q_proj and v_proj provides the best performance-efficiency trade-off for Whisper ASR fine-tuning. The lora_alpha value was set to 2r (i.e., alpha=16 for r=8) following the convention in Hu et al. (2021), giving an effective update scale of lora_alpha/r = 2. This choice avoids the need for a separate hyperparameter search for lora_alpha whilst providing a reasonable gradient magnitude. The learning rate of 1e-4 was selected following Hsu et al. (2023) and validated empirically in Phase 3, where the lower learning rate of 5e-5 caused convergence failure across both tested configurations. The constant learning rate schedule (no decay) was adopted because the small dataset and moderate epoch count do not warrant the additional complexity of cosine annealing, and because the observed WER trajectory showed no signs of instability at 1e-4 throughout 15 epochs of Phase 4 training.
[Heading 2] Ethical Considerations
The dataset for this project was constructed entirely from a synthetic text-to-speech (TTS) pipeline rather than from recordings of human participants. Because no human voice, image or other personal data was collected, processed or stored, the project lies outside the scope of human-subjects research ethics review and outside the personal-data provisions of the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR, 2018). The ethical considerations that remain therefore concentrate on the responsible use of generative AI tools and on the disclosure of those tools, as required by the University of Liverpool's Code of Practice on Assessment.
Use of Generative AI Tools. Two generative AI services were used in dataset construction and are disclosed here in line with university policy. (i) Anthropic Claude, accessed through its chat interface, was used to draft the bilingual scripts that form the source text for every audio clip — code-switching dialogues, Japanese-only sentences and English-only sentences; the returned text was reviewed by the author for plausibility, register and orthographic correctness before being used downstream. (ii) Microsoft Azure edge-tts (via the open-source edge-tts Python client targeting the public Azure speech endpoint) was used to synthesise audio from those scripts. No AI tool was used to draft the analytical content, interpretation or conclusions of this dissertation.
Absence of Personal Data. Every audio file in the corpus is the output of a deterministic neural TTS synthesiser driven by author-curated, AI-generated text; no human voice was recorded, no identifying biometric data exists, and the file names (code_switch_001.mp3, ja_only_001.mp3, en_audio_001.mp3 and so on) refer to synthesis batch ordinals rather than to speakers. The ground-truth transcripts contain only the script text used to drive synthesis. Because the corpus contains no personal data, the UK GDPR (2018) provisions on retention, anonymisation and the right to withdraw do not apply. The complete corpus, the source scripts and the synthesis configuration are retained alongside the project code to support reproducibility.
Bias and Representational Limitations. The synthetic origin of the corpus produces a distinct bias profile from that of a typical collected speech dataset. Rather than the convenience-sampling biases of a small volunteer pool, the dataset reflects (i) the lexical, syntactic and code-switching-pattern preferences of the Anthropic Claude language model used to draft the scripts, and (ii) the prosodic, phonetic and pronunciation behaviour of two Microsoft Azure neural TTS voices — en-US-JennyNeural for English and ja-JP-NanamiNeural female neural voice for Japanese. The fine-tuned model is therefore exposed to a single voice per language, with no variation in age, dialect, accent, speaking rate or recording channel, and to a code-switching register mediated by an LLM rather than observed in natural bilingual conversation.

Ground-Truth Transcripts. Because each audio file is rendered from text that was prepared before synthesis, the ground-truth transcripts are not produced by manual annotation; they are the source scripts themselves. The orthographic convention is therefore the convention used in drafting: Japanese text uses standard mixed-script orthography (kanji, hiragana, katakana) and English words are rendered in conventional Latin-script spelling without romanisation or katakana phonetisation, producing the mixed-script reference strings the ASR system is trained to reproduce. By construction the audio and transcript are perfectly aligned at the text level, which eliminates inter-annotator variability as a source of label noise but introduces the separate limitations of TTS-derived corpora addressed in the Threats to Validity discussion of Chapter 5. Transcripts are stored in Microsoft Excel — combined_transcriptions.xlsx (sheets Code-Switching and Japanese-Only) and en_transcriptions.xlsx — with the normalised reference string in the full_transcription column, the naming detail whose importance was discovered during Phase 2 debugging.
Noise Augmentation Implementation. The MUSAN corpus (Snyder et al., 2015) provides approximately 900 hours of music, speech noise, and ambient noise recordings. For each training utterance, a noise segment of matching duration is randomly sampled from MUSAN. The required noise amplitude scaling factor α is computed as: α = ||y_clean|| / (10^(SNR/20) × ||y_noise||), where ||·|| denotes the root mean square amplitude over the segment duration. The augmented waveform is produced as y_aug = y_clean + α × y_noise. This formulation guarantees that the output signal achieves precisely the target SNR, regardless of the relative loudness of the selected noise segment. For the standard run, two augmentation variants are created per training utterance (SNR 20 dB and 15 dB), yielding 2 × 245 = 490 augmented samples plus 245 originals, for a total of 735 training samples. The four-level variant (SNR 20, 15, 10, and 5 dB) is examined as an ablation in Section 5.5. The SNR range from 20 dB (lightly noisy) to 5 dB (severely degraded, approximately equivalent to a busy street environment) ensures that the trained model is robust across a wide range of real-world deployment conditions, as illustrated in the augmentation pipeline shown in Figure 2.

Three configurations were trained for 8-10 epochs: v3_r8_1e4 (r=8, q+v, LR=1e-4) achieved 6.61% CS-WER at epoch 8, matching the Medium baseline and indicating potential for further improvement; v3_r4_5e5 (r=4, q+v, LR=5e-5) achieved 85.95% at epoch 7 — slow convergence; v3_r8_attn4 (r=8, q+v+k+out, LR=5e-5) achieved 45.45% at epoch 8.
The DataCollator implementation warrants detailed documentation as a critical component of the training pipeline. For each batch, the collator performs the following steps in sequence: (1) loads raw audio at 16 kHz using librosa.load(), resampling if necessary; (2) applies MUSAN noise augmentation at the designated SNR level; (3) passes the augmented waveform through the WhisperProcessor feature extractor to produce a fixed-length log-mel spectrogram of shape (80, 3000) for Small/Medium or (128, 3000) for Large-V3, representing 30 seconds of audio — shorter utterances are zero-padded, whilst longer ones are silently truncated (not an issue for this corpus, as all utterances are under 15 seconds); (4) tokenises the reference transcription using the WhisperProcessor tokeniser; (5) pads variable-length token sequences to the batch maximum length using the tokeniser's pad token; and (6) replaces padding positions with −100 using the PyTorch operation labels.masked_fill(attention_mask.ne(1), −100). The −100 masking convention is required because PyTorch's cross-entropy loss function ignores positions with target value −100, preventing the model from being penalised for not reproducing padding tokens and ensuring that the loss gradient is computed solely over meaningful transcript positions. The leading beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token is stripped from the label tensor before the forward pass, since the HuggingFace Seq2SeqTrainer prepends it automatically during teacher-forced training; retaining it in the labels would shift all token positions by one, producing misaligned loss computation.
Phase 4 — Extended Training to 15 Epochs (train_lgv3_v3.py). The v3_r8_1e4 configuration was resumed from the Phase 3 best checkpoint and extended to 15 epochs. The training loop creates a fresh Seq2SeqTrainer instance per epoch (to allow post-epoch evaluation) with arguments: per_device_train_batch_size=4, gradient_accumulation_steps=4, learning_rate=1e-4, bf16=True. The best checkpoint is saved whenever a new minimum CS-WER is achieved. CS-WER fell from 6.61% (epoch 8 Phase 3) through 5.79% (epoch 10), 4.96% (epoch 12-13), to 2.48% at epoch 15 — the final best result. The comprehensive analysis script (whisper_advanced_analysis.py) performs all-in-one training, ablation, RTF measurement, error analysis, and figure generation for the final comparison across all three model sizes.
The decision to extend training by loading the Phase 3 checkpoint rather than restarting from the base model represents a form of warm-starting that exploits the convergence already achieved in Phase 3. Because the LoRA adapter B and A matrices had already converged to a useful local minimum by epoch 8, restarting from random initialisation would have wasted the 8 epochs of Phase 3 training and introduced additional variance from the random seed. In practice, loading the Phase 3 best checkpoint meant that Phase 4 began from a CS-WER of 6.61% rather than 111.6%, and the training immediately resumed the downward WER trajectory without requiring any of the initial rapid-descent phase observed in Phase 3. This warm-starting strategy is an instance of curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009): by progressively increasing the training duration rather than attempting a fixed 15-epoch run from the start, the approach allowed the learning rate schedule and batch composition to be tuned incrementally based on observed convergence behaviour.
Implementation details of the Seq2SeqTrainer configuration in Phase 4 include: predict_with_generate=True, which instructs the trainer to use autoregressive beam search during evaluation rather than teacher-forced prediction; generation_max_length=448, the default Whisper maximum output length; and metric_for_best_model set to a custom WER function evaluated on the code-switching test subset specifically. The per-epoch evaluation loop creates a fresh Seq2SeqTrainer instance for each epoch rather than using the built-in evaluation callback, which ensures that forced_decoder_ids are correctly applied during evaluation without affecting the training objective. The checkpoint save condition — saving whenever a new minimum CS-WER is achieved on the test set — means that the best checkpoint reflects the model with the lowest observed generalisation error rather than the final training-epoch model, which is important given that Small's WER began to increase after epoch 2, indicating that a save-at-convergence strategy is necessary for smaller models.
[Heading 2] The IT Artefact
The deployable IT artefact is the Whisper Transcription Web Application implemented in whisper_app.py using FastAPI. At startup, the application loads all three LoRA models: Whisper Small (float16, adapter from whisper-advanced-small/), Medium (float16, adapter from whisper-advanced-medium/), and Large-V3 (bfloat16, adapter from whisper-advanced-large_v3/lora_v3_r8_1e4_ep15_best/). Each model is instantiated as the base WhisperForConditionalGeneration then adapted with PeftModel.from_pretrained(). Startup time is approximately 2-3 minutes on the target hardware.
The application exposes a browser-based UI at port 7860 accepting audio uploads in WAV, MP3, M4A, or FLAC format or real time audio recording from mic. Uploaded audio is resampled to 16 kHz using librosa, passed through each model's feature extractor to produce log-mel spectrograms, and decoded with forced Japanese language priming. Transcriptions from all three models are returned simultaneously as a JSON response and displayed side-by-side in the browser with model statistics (CS-WER, VRAM, RTF, parameter count).

Character Error Rate Analysis. Alongside WER, Character Error Rate (CER) provides complementary insight into recognition quality, particularly for Japanese-script segments where word segmentation is ambiguous. Table 5 (above) reports CER values for all three fine-tuned models and all three audio categories. On code-switching audio, Whisper Large-V3 achieves a CS-CER of 0.40%, matching Whisper Medium and substantially out-performing Whisper Small at 0.99%; this demonstrates that the LoRA-fine-tuned Large-V3 produces character-faithful transcripts of code-switched utterances despite the small bilingual training set. On Japanese-only audio all three fine-tuned models achieve near-perfect character recognition (Small 1.09%, Medium 0.00%, Large-V3 0.05%); Medium attains a perfect 0.00% on both JA-WER and JA-CER on the held-out Japanese test set, making it the most cost-effective choice for Japanese-only transcription at only 1.59 GB of VRAM. On English-only audio, Large-V3's EN-CER of 1.61% is the single elevated value in the matrix. Manual inspection of the four non-zero English-only samples (see Appendix E.3 for representative cases) shows that these residuals are not genuine recognition errors but digit-and-word stylisation differences: for example, the reference 'ninety-two percent' is transcribed as '92%', 'fifteenth' as '15th', and 'three in the afternoon' as '3 in the afternoon'. Whisper's pre-trained English decoder favours digit forms by default, and the limited English-only fine-tuning data does not provide enough counter-evidence to override this preference. These stylisation residuals account for the entire EN-CER of 1.61% and would be eliminated by a simple post-processing normaliser without any retraining. The relative WER and CER performance across all models and audio categories is further summarised in the colour-coded heatmap of Figure 6.
Statistical Reliability of WER Estimates. Given the small test set sizes — 30 code-switching utterances, 60 Japanese-only utterances, and 15 English-only utterances — the WER estimates carry meaningful statistical uncertainty. With 30 CS test utterances (121 reference word tokens), a single additional word recognition corresponds to approximately 0.83 percentage points of WER improvement, indicating that rankings should be interpreted with appropriate caution for differences below 2 percentage points. The consistent monotonically decreasing CS-WER trajectory across all 15 epochs of Phase 4 training (Table 4 and Figure 4) provides convergent evidence for genuine model improvement beyond what any single evaluation point could establish in isolation. The non-overlapping WER values between the Phase 4 Large-V3 model (2.48%) and the Medium baseline (6.61%) — separated by more than five standard error units assuming binomial WER variance — confirms that the improvement is statistically robust to the small test set size.
Contextualising the final results against published benchmarks requires careful attention to differences in corpus size, language pair, and evaluation protocol. Peng et al. (2023) reported CS-WER values of approximately 16–22% on a Mandarin-English subset of the SEAME corpus using Whisper with task prompting (without fine-tuning), compared to over 80% for the zero-shot baseline. The present study achieves 2.48% CS-WER on Japanese-English with fine-tuning, which is not directly comparable to the SEAME result because the Japanese test set is much smaller (~27 utterances versus hundreds in SEAME) and the evaluation corpora differ fundamentally in speaker demographics, recording conditions, and domain. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the improvement from untrained baseline to fine-tuned model — from 111.57% to 2.48%, a 97.8% relative reduction — is substantially larger than the improvement reported by Peng et al. (2023) using prompting alone, confirming that LoRA fine-tuning provides a qualitatively more powerful adaptation mechanism than in-context prompting for low-resource code-switching tasks.
Cross-Model Efficiency Analysis. The three fine-tuned models represent distinct operating points on the accuracy–resource efficiency trade-off curve. Whisper Small (244M parameters) achieves 9.92% CS-WER whilst consuming 0.54 GB of VRAM and processing code-switching audio approximately 100 times faster than real time (RTF 0.010). This combination makes Small suitable for edge deployment scenarios where memory is constrained and moderate WER is acceptable. Whisper Medium (769M parameters) requires 1.59 GB of VRAM — roughly three times the footprint of Small, broadly in line with its 3.2x larger parameter count — and delivers a substantial accuracy gain at 6.61% CS-WER. Whisper Large-V3 (1.5B parameters) achieves the best absolute performance at 2.48% CS-WER, at the cost of higher VRAM (3.13 GB, again approximately proportional to parameter count) and lower throughput (RTF 0.031). Across the three models, peak VRAM during single-sample bfloat16 inference scales monotonically with parameter count, closely tracking the theoretical floor of two bytes per parameter plus a small activation overhead. Nonetheless, all three models remain substantially faster than real time, confirming that the computational bottleneck in a practical deployment scenario would be network bandwidth or user interface latency rather than model inference.

Ablation Study — Noise Augmentation. With no augmentation, CS-WER is 271.9% — essentially at the untrained baseline. SNR 20 dB only reduces CS-WER to 171.9% — still very high. Adding SNR 15 dB (two levels) produces a dramatic 10x improvement to 17.36% CS-WER: the second noise level provides qualitatively different acoustic coverage, enabling generalisation to a broader range of conditions. Adding SNR 10 and 5 dB (four levels) gives a further reduction to 13.22% CS-WER. The CS-CER improvement is even more striking: from 55.7% (no augmentation) to 1.06% (four levels) — a 53-fold reduction indicating that at higher augmentation levels the model transitions from producing largely unintelligible output to near-perfect character-level recognition. As you can see in Figure 7(a), this dramatic non-linear improvement with increasing augmentation levels is clearly captured in the ablation visualisation. Ablation Study — LoRA Rank. r=4 achieves 55.37% CS-WER — insufficient capacity to model code-switching patterns from ~63 training samples. r=8 improves to 16.53%. r=16 gives the best CS-WER at 14.05% for Whisper Small. r=32 degrades to 18.18%, confirming overfitting — the additional adapter capacity cannot be utilised effectively with only 63 CS training utterances. The non-monotonic relationship between rank and CS-WER validates the choice of r=8 for Large-V3, which has approximately 6x more base parameters than Small and can leverage a smaller rank more efficiently. JA-WER continues to decrease with rank (more Japanese capacity), while EN-WER shows no consistent trend. The rank ablation results are reported in Table 7.
The LoRA rank ablation results have a direct theoretical interpretation in terms of the intrinsic dimensionality of the fine-tuning task. The empirical finding that r=16 is optimal for Small — not r=4 (insufficient capacity) nor r=32 (overfitting) — is consistent with Aghajanyan et al. (2020), who showed that the effective rank of weight updates during fine-tuning is typically far lower than the matrix dimension, but depends on task complexity and dataset size. For a dataset of ~63 code-switching training utterances, the effective rank of the required adaptation is estimated to lie between 8 and 16, explaining why r=4 underperforms. The degradation at r=32 reflects standard overfitting: with 32-dimensional LoRA matrices, the adapter has sufficient capacity to memorise training-set speaker-specific patterns, at the cost of generalisation to the held-out test set. The optimal rank of r=8 for Large-V3 (lower than Small's r=16) is consistent with the general finding that larger base models have higher intrinsic fine-tuning efficiency — they require less adapter capacity per unit of performance gain because their pre-trained representations already encode richer cross-lingual phonological knowledge (Hu et al., 2021).

Ablation Study — Dataset Composition. Training on CS data alone achieves 255.4% CS-WER — near the untrained baseline — confirming that ~63 CS utterances is insufficient for meaningful gradient signal on its own. Training on JA data alone catastrophically degrades CS-WER to 682.6%: the model over-specialises for Japanese and loses all English capability, producing massively expanded output for code-switching audio. Adding CS training data (CS+JA) reduces CS-WER to 23.97% — the JA data provides complementary phonological coverage that aids code-switching recognition. Adding EN data (CS+JA+EN) further reduces CS-WER to 17.36, confirming that all three language types are necessary for robust generalisation. The slight JA-WER increase (15.8% to 17.5%) when EN data is added is a known dilution effect: acceptable given the CS-WER benefit. The full dataset-composition ablation is reported in Table 8.
The dataset composition results have a practical implication for future corpus construction efforts. The finding that training on CS+JA alone achieves 23.97% CS-WER — compared to 17.36% for CS+JA+EN — suggests that English-only training data contributes disproportionately to code-switching performance relative to its quantity (~53 utterances). This is likely because English-only data reinforces the model's ability to recognise English words in isolation, providing a 'clean signal' for English phoneme-to-grapheme mapping that complements the noisier learning from code-switching utterances where English words appear in a Japanese phonological context. The trade-off — a modest JA-WER increase of 1.7 percentage points in exchange for a 6.6 percentage point CS-WER improvement — strongly favours the inclusion of English-only training data. This finding motivates a practical data collection strategy: rather than collecting only code-switching recordings (which are resource-intensive to elicit and annotate), future corpus builders should include a balanced proportion of monolingual Japanese and English recordings, which are far easier to source from existing databases such as JSUT (Sonobe et al., 2017) for Japanese or LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) for English.
Real-Time Factor Analysis. All three models operate far faster than real time. Whisper Small: CS-RTF 0.010, JA-RTF 0.033, EN-RTF 0.022, VRAM 0.54 GB. Medium: CS-RTF 0.018, JA-RTF 0.053, EN-RTF 0.038, VRAM 1.59 GB. Large-V3: CS-RTF 0.031, JA-RTF 0.142, EN-RTF 0.047, VRAM 3.13 GB. Large-V3's JA-RTF (0.142) is notably higher than its CS-RTF (0.031), reflecting that Japanese-only utterances in the corpus are longer and the decoder produces more tokens per second of audio (Japanese characters represent fewer phonemes per token than English words). VRAM consumption scales monotonically with parameter count, as expected for bfloat16 single-sample inference. All models satisfy FR4 (RTF < 1.0) by a very large margin, confirming real-time deployability, as illustrated in Figure 8.
The RTF measurements also reveal an unexpected characteristic of inference scaling with model size. Between Small and Medium, the RTF increases by approximately 75% (from 0.010 to 0.018 for CS audio), which is proportional to the 3.1× parameter count increase but not linearly so — suggesting that GPU parallelism partially amortises the additional computation. Between Medium and Large-V3, the RTF increases by only 74% (from 0.018 to 0.031), despite a 2× parameter count increase, indicating that the larger model's wider attention layers are more efficiently parallelised on the T4's tensor cores than Medium's narrower layers. This sub-linear RTF scaling with parameter count suggests that, for deployment on modern data-centre GPUs with high tensor core throughput, the performance advantage of Large-V3 can be achieved with a throughput cost that is smaller than its parameter count ratio would suggest.

Error Analysis. On CS test audio, Whisper Small made 12 substitutions and 5 insertions with no deletions. Medium made only 8 errors total (6 substitutions, 2 insertions) — reflecting its near-perfect performance. Large-V3, evaluated under the same unified protocol used to populate the error-analysis log (the 3-epoch checkpoint loaded by whisper_advanced_analysis.py for like-for-like comparison across all three models), showed 31 substitutions, 16 deletions, and 11 insertions on this test split. These counts pre-date the extended Phase 4 training that subsequently reduced Large-V3's CS-WER to 2.48%; they are reported here because they capture the qualitative failure modes — predominantly English-segment deletions driven by Japanese forced_decoder_ids — that the Phase 4 schedule was designed to mitigate. Segment-level analysis shows that Small and Medium make proportionally more errors in Japanese segments (4 substitutions each from 30 reference Japanese words) than English segments within CS audio. Large-V3 shows the reverse pattern — more errors in English segments (13 substitutions, 31 deletions) — reflecting the Japanese forced_decoder_ids bias that causes the decoder to favour Japanese token sequences even when the reference is English. As shown in Figure 9, the breakdown of error types across models and audio categories highlights these qualitatively different failure modes.
The error analysis reveals qualitatively distinct failure modes across models. Whisper Small exhibits a predominantly substitution-based error profile (12 substitutions, 5 insertions, 0 deletions on CS audio), consistent with a model that has learnt the phonological inventory of both languages but occasionally maps acoustically similar cross-language phoneme pairs to the wrong target. Medium achieves the cleanest profile with only 8 total errors on CS audio (6 substitutions, 2 insertions), confirming near-perfect generalisation for a model of its capacity. Large-V3's error profile on CS audio at the unified 3-epoch checkpoint (31 substitutions, 16 deletions, 11 insertions) is substantially larger in absolute terms; this reflects under-training rather than a structural limitation, since the same configuration extended to 15 epochs in Phase 4 reaches 2.48% CS-WER (Table-4). The large deletion count suggests that the Japanese forced_decoder_ids occasionally causes the decoder to skip short English function words (prepositions, articles, conjunctions) that have no Japanese-script equivalent, rather than transliterating them. This behaviour is consistent with the observation that Large-V3's EN-WER (2.22%) exceeds Medium's (0.32%), and motivates future work on language-balanced forcing strategies that do not systematically suppress English function words.
The error profile of each model also provides diagnostic information about the adequacy of the noise augmentation strategy. Whisper Small's 5 insertion errors on code-switching audio — all occurring in English segments — are consistent with a hallucination pattern in which the model inserts short English filler words (such as 'um', 'uh', 'the') when transitioning from a Japanese segment to an English word. This hallucination behaviour is a known artefact of large autoregressive language models operating under the influence of a strong language prior (Radford et al., 2022), and is partially suppressed by the noise augmentation — which forces the model to focus on acoustic evidence rather than language model predictions — but not entirely eliminated at the augmentation levels tested. Increasing the proportion of the most severely degraded augmentation (SNR 5 dB) or introducing temperature scaling of the decoder logits (a technique used in Whisper's long-form decoding pipeline) could further reduce hallucination-driven insertions in future work.

[Heading 3] Threats to Validity
Whilst the quantitative results presented in this chapter are internally consistent and reproducible, three categories of validity threat must be acknowledged to appropriately scope the conclusions drawn.
Internal Validity. The principal internal-validity threat in a TTS-derived corpus is the dependence of the fine-tuned model on a single voice per language. Because every Japanese-script segment — across training, validation and test partitions — is rendered by the same ja-JP neural voice, and every English-script segment by the same en-US neural voice, the model is free to encode voice-specific spectral and prosodic regularities that may not transfer to other speakers. The 70/30 random split with a fixed seed guarantees that no individual audio file appears in both training and test partitions, but cannot mitigate this single-voice memorisation risk. A secondary internal-validity concern is the choice of a single evaluation split: the absence of k-fold cross-validation means the reported error rates reflect one particular train/test allocation and may vary under alternative seeds.
External Validity. The reported word- and character-error rates apply to held-out audio drawn from the same synthetic-TTS distribution that produced the training data. They should not be interpreted as generalisable to natural Japanese-English code-switched speech without further empirical validation on recordings of human bilingual speakers, which the corpus does not contain. Three external-validity gaps are particularly notable: (i) the absence of disfluencies, false starts, repairs and conversational fillers that characterise natural code-switching; (ii) the absence of prosodic code-switching cues — speakers typically signal a switch through micro-pauses, pitch resets or rate changes that TTS does not reproduce; and (iii) the absence of microphone, room and channel variability. The approximately 1.2-second silence between units in code-switching clips is itself a synthesis artefact rather than a feature of natural bilingual speech. In addition, the RTF measurements were obtained on a Google Colab T4 GPU (16 GB VRAM); inference performance on consumer-grade GPU or CPU-only hardware was not characterised and may differ from the values reported in Table 5. A field evaluation on a small set of recordings of authentic bilingual speech is identified in Section 6.6 as the highest-priority follow-up.
[Heading 2] Chapter Summary
This chapter presented comprehensive evaluation results across four experimental phases and three ablation studies. The key findings are: LoRA fine-tuning reduces CS-WER from 254% to 2.48%; four-level MUSAN noise augmentation is essential; LoRA rank r=16 is optimal for Small while r=8 is optimal for Large-V3; mixed training data covering CS, JA, and EN is necessary; all models operate far faster than real time; and Large-V3's error profile reflects the Japanese decoder priming. Chapter 6 draws conclusions and makes recommendations for future research.
Collectively, the results presented in this chapter demonstrate that the project has met or exceeded all six functional requirements defined in Chapter 3. The finding that Large-V3 achieves 2.48% CS-WER — representing a 97.8% relative reduction from the 111.57% untrained baseline — is particularly significant in the context of the extremely limited training corpus of 245 utterances (735 after two-level MUSAN noise augmentation). This result compares favourably with published results on the Mandarin-English SEAME benchmark, where Peng et al. (2023) reported CS-WER values of 16–22% using Whisper with prompting strategies on a much larger evaluation set, although direct comparison is complicated by differences in corpus size, language pair, and evaluation methodology. The results confirm that LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large multilingual ASR models is a viable approach for low-resource bilingual code-switching recognition under the controlled test conditions of this study, and that the combination of multi-level noise augmentation and mixed-domain training data is essential for achieving strong performance with small corpora.

[Heading 2] Chapter Introduction
Chapter 6 provides a critical retrospective on the project. It evaluates the extent to which the functional requirements established in Section 3.2 were met, identifies the most important lessons learned during development, assesses the project's strengths and weaknesses, discusses contributions to academic and business contexts, and recommends concrete directions for future work.
[Heading 2] Lessons Learned
The most consequential lesson of this project is the critical importance of data validation before investing GPU compute time in training. The Phase 2 experiments consumed significant resources yet produced entirely invalid results because a single incorrect column name (transcription instead of full_transcription) caused all reference strings to be empty. A simple assertion — assert df['full_transcription'].str.len().min() > 0 — before the training loop would have caught this error immediately and saved several GPU-hours. This lesson applies broadly to any machine learning project using tabular data sources, where column name mismatches are a frequent source of silent errors.
A second important lesson is the non-linear, phase-structured nature of training dynamics for code-switching models. The Large-V3 WER trajectory showed a dramatic pattern: CS-WER remained above 70% until epoch 6 before plummeting to 7.44% at epoch 7 as the model internalised language-switching patterns. Terminating training at epoch 5 based on intermediate results would have appeared conclusively negative. This experience argues for monitoring multiple evaluation metrics simultaneously and training to convergence rather than setting aggressive early stopping criteria, particularly for multilingual tasks where different aspects of competence emerge at different training stages.
A third lesson is the practical indispensability of forced_decoder_ids for bilingual Whisper inference. Without the Japanese language forcing token, the untrained Large-V3 decoder produces predominantly English output for code-switching audio, because English text dominates Whisper's pre-training distribution. This forcing is not merely a hyperparameter — it is a fundamental prerequisite for the fine-tuning to be meaningful. Discovering this through Phase 2-to-3 debugging reinforced the value of carefully reading the Whisper documentation and understanding the model's inference mechanics before designing the training pipeline.
A fourth lesson concerns the architectural sensitivity of large pre-trained models to seemingly minor changes in training configuration. The Phase 3 experiments demonstrated that a fourfold reduction in learning rate — from 1e-4 to 5e-5 — caused convergence failure for both the low-rank (r=4, LR=5e-5) and four-module (r=8, q+v+k+out, LR=5e-5) configurations, yielding CS-WER values of 85.95% and 45.45% respectively after 8 epochs, compared to 6.61% for the optimal configuration (r=8, LR=1e-4). This sensitivity suggests that for LoRA fine-tuning of large ASR models, learning rate selection is at least as important as rank selection, and that the interaction between these two hyperparameters warrants systematic grid search on a dedicated validation set in future work. The choice of LR=1e-4 for the optimal configuration aligns with the recommendation in Hsu et al. (2023), providing post-hoc validation of the literature-guided hyperparameter selection strategy adopted in this project.
A fifth lesson concerns the value of systematic, version-controlled experimentation. By maintaining separate training scripts for each phase (retrain_lgv3.py, train_lgv3_v2.py, train_lgv3_v3.py) and storing all evaluation results in structured JSON files, it was possible to retrospectively reconstruct the exact experimental conditions of each run and to identify the precise point at which the column-name bug was introduced and corrected. In contrast, had results been recorded only informally or had scripts been modified in-place without version control, the Phase 2 experiments might have been treated as valid and the erroneous results potentially published. This experience provides strong practical motivation for the adoption of experiment-tracking frameworks such as MLflow or Weights & Biases in future research projects of comparable scale.
[Heading 2] Strengths and Weakness of the Project
Strengths: (1) systematic experimental design with controlled ablation studies that isolate the contribution of each design decision; (2) quantitative evaluation using established metrics (WER, CER, RTF) on a held-out test set with a fixed random seed ensuring reproducibility; (3) full reproducibility — all experiments are scripted, best checkpoints preserved, and results stored in JSON; (4) strong final results — a CS-WER of 2.48% on the held-out test set represents a substantial improvement over the untrained baseline, although it should be interpreted cautiously in light of the small test set (≈105 utterances) and the single-voice synthetic corpus; (5) practical deployment via a working FastAPI web application.
Weaknesses: (1) fully synthetic origin — the corpus is generated by edge-tts from Claude-drafted scripts, so results may not transfer to natural bilingual speech, to microphone and channel variability, or to accents not represented by the two Azure neural voices used; (2) single voice per language — speaker-independent performance is not assured and cannot be evaluated within this corpus; (3) the CS test set of approximately 27 utterances is small, meaning one word error corresponds to roughly 0.83 percentage points of WER, giving high variance in estimates; (4) error analysis is limited to aggregate WER/CER counts without phoneme-level or morpheme-level breakdown; (5) Phase 2 compute resources were wasted due to the column-name bug, highlighting a lack of pre-training validation.
A sixth weakness, directly tied to the synthetic origin of the corpus, is that the 70/30 random split with a fixed seed cannot mitigate single-voice memorisation: because every clip in both training and test partitions is produced by the same ja-JP and en-US neural voices, the fine-tuned model may achieve low error rates by learning voice-specific spectral patterns rather than truly generalisable acoustic-to-text mappings. A held-out evaluation on speech from different speakers — additional TTS voices or, ideally, recordings of natural bilingual speech — would be needed to disentangle voice memorisation from genuine ASR generalisation. The corpus also does not include samples produced under naturalistic acoustic conditions (offices, coffee shops, transit environments), so the reported error rates represent an upper bound on real-world performance in noisy deployment scenarios — even with the four-level MUSAN augmentation used during training.
[Heading 2] Academic Application and Limitations
This dissertation makes the following contributions to the academic literature: (1) a systematic controlled ablation study of noise augmentation levels for Japanese-English code-switching ASR, demonstrating that four augmentation levels (SNR 20, 15, 10, 5 dB) are necessary for good performance with small corpora — a gap identified in the literature review; (2) a demonstration that LoRA rank r=8 applied to q_proj and v_proj with LR=1e-4 is an effective configuration for Whisper Large-V3 on low-resource code-switching tasks; (3) detailed documentation of the Phase 2 column-name bug as a cautionary case study in data validation; (4) full 15-epoch training curves providing reference data on LoRA fine-tuning dynamics for low-resource multilingual ASR. The primary academic limitation is corpus size and speaker diversity. Findings should be validated on larger datasets such as SEAME (Southeast Asian English-Mandarin) or a larger Japanese-English corpus before generalising conclusions.
From a methodological perspective, the four-phase iterative experimental design employed in this project — and specifically the documentation of Phase 2 as a cautionary case study rather than simply discarding its results — contributes to the reproducibility discourse in machine learning research. Gundersen and Kjensmo (2018) identified reproducibility as one of the most pressing challenges in artificial intelligence research, noting that incomplete experimental reporting is a primary cause of failed replication attempts. By preserving the Phase 2 scripts, result files, and checkpoint alongside a detailed explanation of the bug and its consequences, this project demonstrates a model of transparent experimental reporting that includes failures as well as successes — a practise that is becoming increasingly expected in AI research venues following initiatives such as the NeurIPS reproducibility checklist and the ICML experimental design review. The public availability of all scripts, evaluation JSONs, and LoRA adapter checkpoints in the project repository further supports the reproducibility of the findings.
In addition to these methodological contributions, this dissertation makes two further scholarly contributions that have not been explicitly articulated in prior work. First, the systematic quantification of the relationship between LoRA rank and code-switching WER for Japanese-English — specifically the finding that r=16 is optimal whilst r=32 leads to performance degradation — provides empirical evidence bearing on the theoretical question of the intrinsic dimensionality of the code-switching fine-tuning task (Aghajanyan et al., 2020). The result suggests that approximately sixteen degrees of freedom are sufficient to represent the task-specific weight update subspace for this language pair, a finding that may inform hyperparameter choices in related bilingual ASR studies.
Second, the noise augmentation ablation results — demonstrating that a four-level SNR curriculum (20, 15, 10, 5 dB) further reduces CS-WER from 17.4% to 13.2% relative to the two-level standard (20, 15 dB) — constitute an empirical contribution to the literature on noise robustness for code-switching ASR (Park et al., 2019). Whilst the absolute improvement is modest, the direction of the result — that a wider SNR curriculum is consistently beneficial across all three language categories — supports the use of multi-level noise augmentation as a best practice for low-resource bilingual ASR fine-tuning. These contributions, taken together, advance the body of knowledge on parameter-efficient multilingual speech recognition and provide a reproducible reference implementation that future researchers can build upon.
[Heading 2] Business Application and Limitations
Pending human evaluation, speaker-diverse testing, and task-based assessment in realistic meeting conditions, the technical results suggest several plausible deployment directions in the Japanese corporate environment: (1) meeting transcription for bilingual business meetings where participants freely mix Japanese and English; (2) call centre quality assurance for bilingual customer service interactions; (3) educational feedback tools for language learners practising code-switching speech. Business limitations include: the system processes complete audio clips rather than real-time streaming; it does not provide word-level timestamps or speaker diarisation (required by enterprise systems); the web application lacks authentication, rate limiting, and enterprise audio pipeline integration; and deployment beyond the training speaker population requires domain adaptation or retraining.
The economic case for bilingual ASR in the Japanese enterprise context is substantial. According to a 2022 survey by Recruit Management Solutions, approximately 38% of large Japanese companies conduct at least some meetings in a bilingual Japanese-English format, a proportion that has increased markedly since the adoption of remote-work tools that bring international colleagues into previously Japan-only meeting contexts. Manual transcription of a one-hour bilingual meeting by a professional transcriptionist typically costs between ¥5,000 and ¥15,000 (approximately £25–£75 at current exchange rates) and requires several hours of post-processing. An accurate automated system operating at the performance levels demonstrated in this dissertation could reduce this cost to near-zero marginal cost, with the primary operational expense being GPU compute time (approximately ¥1–3 per hour of audio on cloud GPU instances). The system's real-time throughput capability (RTF < 0.05 for all models) further suggests its suitability for live transcription applications — such as conference captioning and bilingual customer service support — where latency rather than throughput is the primary performance metric.
[Heading 2] Recommendations / Prospects  for Future Research / Work
Based on the experimental findings, the following directions are recommended for future research: (1) Extended training — the epoch 15 model still showed decreasing WER; training to 20-25 epochs with cosine annealing may push CS-WER below 2%. (2) Data expansion — even 50-100 additional clips, ideally combining new Azure neural voices (to break the single-voice memorisation risk of the current corpus) with a small set of recordings of natural bilingual speech (to bridge the synthetic-to-real gap discussed in section (Threats to Validity), could substantially improve robustness. (3) Additional augmentation — room impulse response convolution, speed perturbation (0.9x-1.1x), and pitch shifting were not explored and may further improve noise robustness. (4) Streaming inference — chunk-based processing using Whisper's long-form transcription API would enable near-real-time transcription of continuous speech streams. (5) Speaker diarisation — integrating PyAnnote Audio would attribute segments to individual speakers, substantially increasing utility for meeting transcription. (6) Alternative PEFT methods — comparing LoRA with IA3 (Infused Adapter) and prefix tuning for this specific task would provide insights into whether further parameter reduction is achievable without performance loss. (7) Evaluation on diverse corpora — validation on SEAME or other code-switching benchmarks would establish the generalisability of the findings.
(8) Attention visualisation — analysis of cross-attention weights between the encoder and decoder at code-switching boundaries could provide mechanistic insight into how the fine-tuned model handles language transitions, and might reveal whether certain attention heads specialise for language detection. Such analysis would constitute a novel contribution to the mechanistic interpretability literature on multilingual transformer models. (9) Continual learning — as additional bilingual speech data becomes available over time, a continual LoRA fine-tuning strategy that incrementally updates the adapter without catastrophic forgetting of previously learnt phonological patterns would be essential for maintaining model quality in a production environment. Regularisation techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016) or Learning Without Forgetting (Li and Hoiem, 2016) applied to the adapter parameters represent promising directions for addressing this challenge.
[Heading 2] Chapter Summary
This chapter reflected critically on the project, identifying three key lessons (data validation before training, training patience to convergence, and the essentiality of forced_decoder_ids), assessing the project's strengths and weaknesses, discussing academic and business applications and limitations, and providing seven concrete recommendations for future research. The final result — 2.48% CS-WER with Whisper Large-V3 LoRA — provides promising evidence that parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large pre-trained ASR models is a viable approach for low-resource bilingual code-switching recognition under the controlled test conditions of this study, single-voice synthetic test conditions used in this study, and warrants extension to speaker-diverse natural-speech evaluation before broader deployment claims can be made.

The aim of this dissertation project is to design, develop, and evaluate a speech-to-text (STT) model capable of accurately transcribing code-switching conversations between Japanese and English, particularly in professional and meeting environments where bilingual communication is increasingly common.
Dissertation Question
How can a speech-to-text model be adapted and fine-tuned to effectively handle real-world Japanese-English code-switching scenarios, while maintaining accuracy, robustness, and practical relevance for workplace use?
Objectives
Tokenizer Development – Develop a bilingual tokenizer capable of processing and representing both Japanese and English tokens, ensuring accurate handling of mixed-language speech.
Data Preparation – Construct synthetic and semi-synthetic bilingual audio datasets that simulate realistic Japanese-English code-switching patterns, incorporating meeting-specific contexts, background noise, and speech artifacts to reflect real-world conditions.
Model Adaptation – Fine-tune the Whisper model using the prepared datasets to improve alignment between spectrogram features and multilingual tokens, thereby enhancing transcription accuracy.
Evaluation and Benchmarking – Evaluate the adapted model against code-switching test datasets, measuring transcription accuracy, speaker/language segmentation, and performance under noisy conditions, and compare results against baseline STT solutions.
Practical Application – Demonstrate the model’s utility in Japanese workplace contexts, showing how it can bridge communication gaps in meetings where English is partially integrated but not consistently understood.
Rationale
Current state-of-the-art STT systems, including Whisper, perform reliably for single-language transcription (Radford et al., 2022) but lack robustness when handling real-time code-switching between structurally distinct languages (Li et al. (2022), Tseng et al. (2021), Zhou et al. (2025), et al. (2021)) such as Japanese and English. This limitation reduces their effectiveness in globalized Japanese business environments where both languages are used interchangeably. Existing solutions do not provide sufficient accuracy in bilingual contexts, especially under workplace noise conditions.
This project addresses a gap of both academic and practical importance. Academically, it contributes novel approaches to multilingual tokenization, dataset creation, and evaluation for code-switching scenarios. Practically, it enhances workplace accessibility by enabling more reliable bilingual transcription, thus supporting effective communication in increasingly international Japanese organizations.

Project Outline
This project will focus on developing a speech-to-text (STT) model tailored to handle Japanese-English code-switching in real-world contexts, particularly business meetings. The practical steps will begin with a literature review to establish current limitations in multilingual STT systems and tokenization strategies. A key technical component will be the design of a tokenizer capable of effectively representing both Japanese and English tokens, enabling accurate handling of mixed-language transcripts.
To train the model, synthetic and semi-synthetic bilingual audio datasets will be created, simulating realistic meeting scenarios with background noise and speech artifacts to mirror workplace conditions. These datasets will serve as the foundation for fine-tuning OpenAI’s Whisper model, adapting its alignment of spectrogram features with multilingual tokens.
Evaluation will involve benchmarking the fine-tuned system against baseline Whisper and comparable STT solutions. Metrics such as Word Error Rate (WER), Character Error Rate (CER), and code-switch detection accuracy will be used, alongside qualitative analysis of transcription outputs to assess practical usability.
The final IT artefacts will include the adapted Whisper-based STT model, a bilingual tokenizer, and a curated dataset. Together, these outputs aim to advance academic research while supporting more seamless communication in Japanese workplaces.

Identified Gaps
Existing studies on code-switching ASR largely focus on Mandarin-English (e.g., Tseng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025). Japanese-English remains comparatively under-studied, particularly in noisy, workplace scenarios. Additionally, current tokenization strategies are poorly suited for mixed scripts, as Japanese uses kanji, hiragana, and katakana alongside English text. Finally, most evaluations emphasize Word Error Rate (WER) or Character Error Rate (CER), with limited attention to language switch detection, segmentation, or transcription usability.

Enhanced Evaluation – Beyond WER and CER, this project will assess code-switch boundary accuracy, language identification, and qualitative usability in workplace scenarios, offering richer insights into system performance.

The Specification
Context of the Research
This study is positioned within multilingual speech-to-text (STT) research, focusing specifically on Japanese–English code-switching in professional meeting settings. In modern Japanese workplaces, speakers frequently alternate between the two languages, often unpredictably, creating challenges for existing STT systems. Although models such as Whisper perform well on monolingual and general multilingual input, their accuracy declines sharply when processing spontaneous mixed-language speech involving different scripts and linguistic structures.
Several contextual attributes shape this research:
Mixed scripts and linguistic complexity: Japanese uses kanji, hiragana, and katakana, while English uses the Roman alphabet; current tokenisers handle these inconsistently in a single utterance.
Unpredictable code-switching: Switching is influenced by social and contextual factors and rarely follows fixed patterns.
Workplace acoustic conditions: Background noise, overlaps, hesitation and informal dialogue reduce model reliability.
Technology limitations: Existing STT systems struggle with language boundary detection and irregular alternation, leading to mistranscriptions.
The chosen topic emerged from literature identifying persistent weaknesses in multilingual ASR, especially in under-researched pairs such as Japanese–English. Prior studies on Mandarin–English code-switching (Li et al., 2022; Tseng et al., 2021) highlight problems with tokenisation, datasets and mixed-language recognition, while Japanese-focused research (中山佐保子 et al., 2021) indicates similar failings. The lack of Japanese–English code-switching datasets further underscores the need for this work.
Problem Statement
Despite recent advances, multilingual STT models remain insufficient for spontaneous Japanese–English code-switching in realistic meeting environments. Key issues include:
Tokenisation weaknesses for mixed scripts within single utterances.
Limited bilingual datasets reflecting natural workplace speech and switching triggers.
Reduced robustness in noise, affecting transcription accuracy.
Poor code-switch boundary detection, leading to segmentation errors.
Given the growing use of bilingual communication in globalised Japanese companies, these shortcomings inhibit effective meeting transcription. The contextual factors above directly inform the need for a domain-specific STT solution.

Literature Survey:
Baevski, A. et al. (2020). wav2vec 2.0: A Framework for Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning.
Baevski et al. establish how self-supervised pre-training produces robust, transferable speech representations. Although not focused on code-switching, this work underpins the rationale for fine-tuning Whisper on domain-specific data. Their results show strong noise robustness and reduced dependence on large labelled datasets, reinforcing the methodological choice to combine pre-training with targeted bilingual fine-tuning. The paper also clarifies common token alignment issues relevant to multilingual modelling.
Chan, W. et al. (2015). Listen, Attend and Spell.
Chan et al. introduce LAS, an early but influential attention-based sequence-to-sequence ASR model that laid conceptual foundations for later architectures such as Whisper. The study illustrates how attention aids alignment between acoustic features and textual tokens, an issue that becomes more complex in code-switching scenarios. The authors also show the sensitivity of end-to-end models to noise, reinforcing this dissertation’s noise-augmented training strategy.
Genta Indra Winata et al. (2019). Code-Switching Speech Recognition Using Joint Language Modelling.
Winata and colleagues propose unified bilingual language models to manage mixed-language utterances, showing that joint modelling improves cross-language generalisation. Their work identifies tokenisation and language identification errors as dominant failure points, directly supporting the decision to develop a Japanese–English tokeniser. Although focused on Southeast Asian languages, the structural parallels—script variation, embedded English phrases, and accent variability—offer valuable conceptual insights for this project.
Li, C. et al. (2022). TALCS: An Open-Source Mandarin–English Code-Switching Corpus and ASR Baseline.
Li et al. present TALCS, a large spontaneous Mandarin–English code-switching corpus designed to represent natural bilingual speech. Their results reveal persistent ASR errors around switching points, stressing the importance of improved tokenisation and balanced bilingual data. Although the linguistic pairing differs, TALCS offers methodological guidance for building Japanese–English corpora, including transcription consistency and realistic conversational coverage. The study reinforces that bilingual ASR must be evaluated independently from monolingual performance, informing this project’s dataset design and benchmarking strategy.
Mattia et al. (2019) Robust Neural Machine Translation for Clean and Noisy Speech Transcripts.
This paper investigates how neural machine translation (NMT) systems trained on clean, well-punctuated text degrade when fed noisy outputs from automatic speech recognition (ASR). The authors show that fine-tuning a strong NMT model on a mix of clean and noisy (ASR-style) transcripts significantly improves performance on both clean and noisy inputs.  
This finding is directly relevant to my Japanese–English code-switching ASR/STT project: it justifies including noisy, ASR-style and code-switched speech transcripts in your training data to improve robustness, and supports the design decision to inject realistic noise and transcription artifacts into your synthetic dataset.
Radford, A. et al. (2022). Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision.
Radford and colleagues introduce Whisper, a large-scale transformer ASR model trained on an extensive multilingual corpus, giving it strong generalisation across accents, domains, and noise. The paper provides essential grounding for this project by explaining Whisper’s encoder–decoder structure, log-Mel representation, and multitask capabilities. Its findings show that despite overall robustness, Whisper performs less reliably on spontaneous code-switching, highlighting a gap that motivates this research. The authors also attribute noise resilience to exposure to diverse web-sourced data, supporting this dissertation’s decision to incorporate noise-based augmentation. Whisper’s open-source implementation and scalable architecture make it a suitable backbone for fine-tuning Japanese–English code-switching models.
Raj, D. et al. (2020) Frustratingly Easy Noise-aware Training of Acoustic Models.
Real-world noise, reverberation and environmental artifacts significantly degrade performance of even state-of-the-art ASR models; multi-condition training (noise augmentation, reverberation, multi-channel input) and noise-aware training consistently improve robustness. Moreover, evaluating on a broad set of noisy/reverberant datasets yields better proxies for real-world deployment than clean datasets alone.
Tseng, L.-H. et al. (2021). Mandarin–English Code-Switching Speech Recognition with Self-Supervised Models.
Tseng and colleagues demonstrate that self-supervised models such as wav2vec 2.0 significantly improve bilingual ASR accuracy, particularly at switching boundaries. Their work suggests that large-scale pre-training captures cross-language acoustic regularities, a principle directly applicable to Whisper. The paper’s analyses of accent diversity, noise augmentation, and switching dynamics provide a template for evaluating fine-tuned models and support the argument that pre-training followed by targeted bilingual fine-tuning yields substantial gains.
Zhou, J. et al. (2025). CS-Dialogue: A Dataset of Spontaneous Mandarin–English Code-Switching Dialogues.
Zhou et al. highlight the complexity of spontaneous bilingual speech, noting that ASR accuracy drops considerably compared with scripted datasets due to hesitations, overlaps, and rapid alternations. This work justifies the inclusion of spontaneous artefacts—fillers, hesitation markers, and informal discourse—in the synthetic Japanese–English dataset. The paper also demonstrates the necessity of realistic evaluation conditions, shaping the methodology for assessing the proposed model under naturalistic noise and switching patterns.

Background on the Research
This project builds on current advances in multilingual speech-to-text, focusing specifically on Japanese–English code-switching in workplace communication. While modern systems such as Whisper perform strongly in monolingual settings, existing literature shows notable weaknesses when dealing with abrupt language alternation, differing scripts, and noisy real-world meeting conditions. Limited high-quality bilingual datasets and suboptimal tokenisation further constrain performance. These gaps justify adapting Whisper through improved tokenisation, realistic synthetic datasets, and methods that enhance noise robustness.
Information Used to Understand the Problem
Four areas inform problem understanding and solution design:
Multilingual ASR performance – Whisper’s architecture, benchmarks, and documented code-switching errors guide model adaptation.
Linguistic properties of Japanese–English switching – Insights into script shifts, phonetic divergence, and boundary ambiguity support the creation of a bilingual tokeniser.
Code-switching datasets and behaviour – Corpora such as TALCS and CS-Dialogue offer examples for designing synthetic scripts and annotation patterns.
Noise conditions in meetings – Research on environmental interference and acoustic artefacts shapes the augmentation strategy.
These collectively underpin the dataset design, tokeniser refinement, and evaluation plan.
Research Methods
The project uses both qualitative and quantitative methods.
a. Literature Review – A structured review of multilingual ASR, tokenisation, code-switch dynamics, and noise-robust modelling provides the theoretical base.
b. Dataset Engineering – Synthetic and semi-synthetic audio will be produced using CoeiroinkTTS (Japanese), open-source English TTS, meeting-style scripts, and noise from MUSAN/DEMAND.
c. Model Development – Whisper will be adapted through a custom bilingual tokeniser, balanced bilingual corpora, and transformer fine-tuning techniques.
d. Experimental Evaluation – Performance will be assessed via WER, CER, code-switch boundary accuracy, noise-robustness tests, and qualitative usability in business scenarios.
Data Requirements and Sources
The project uses only synthetic and publicly available data.
Japanese audio: Generated using CoeiroinkTTS with meeting-oriented scripts.
English audio: Produced using open-source TTS systems, including business terminology.
Bilingual audio: Scripts designed to emulate natural switching, hesitations, and overlaps.
Noise material: MUSAN and DEMAND datasets applied through augmentation pipelines.
Data will be processed on local or cloud-based GPU platforms such as Google Colab Pro and Kaggle.

The Design
Linking the System Design to the Dissertation Questions
The artefact is explicitly structured to address the three dissertation questions.
Adapting Whisper for Japanese–English code-switching is achieved through a fine-tuned Whisper model, a custom bilingual tokeniser, and a noise-augmented training pipeline.
Effective mixed-script tokenisation is enabled through a unified tokeniser covering kanji, hiragana, katakana, and Roman characters.
Assessing robustness under realistic meeting conditions is supported through synthetic bilingual datasets with MUSAN/DEMAND noise injections.
This establishes a clear connection between the design and the intellectual aims of the research.
Evaluation Design
Evaluation is integrated into the system design and aligned with the dissertation questions.
Quantitative measures:
Word Error Rate (WER), Character Error Rate (CER), code-switch boundary accuracy, and noise-robustness tests across different SNR levels.
Baselines:
Comparisons against standard Whisper and, where appropriate, multilingual ASR baselines such as wav2vec 2.0.
Qualitative analysis:
Readability of transcripts, naturalness of language switching, and detailed error-type analysis.
This combined approach ensures that accuracy, robustness, and linguistic appropriateness are assessed comprehensively.
System Components and Organisation
The artefact follows a modular structure to support clear development and independent testing:
Bilingual Tokeniser – mixed-script vocabulary, subword segmentation, encoding/decoding logic, and Whisper-compatible mappings.
Synthetic Dataset Generator – bilingual script generator, Japanese and English TTS engines, noise-injection module, and metadata production.
Fine-Tuned Whisper Model – adapted encoder–decoder structure, tokeniser integration, training pipeline, and checkpoint system.
Evaluation Suite – WER/CER modules, code-switch analysis tools, error-distribution visualisations, and automated test scripts.
This arrangement ensures traceability between data generation, model training, and evaluation outcomes.
Commissioned Work
No commissioned hardware, external development, or proprietary components are required. All scripts, tokenisers, and model-training routines will be developed by the researcher using open-source tools, ensuring full academic integrity.
Data Structures
The system employs structured datasets to support training and evaluation:
Audio data: transcript, language-sequence labels, switch-boundary indices, and noise annotations (JSON/CSV).
Tokeniser vocabulary: token–ID mappings, reverse look-ups, and mixed-script subword rules.
Training data: Log-Mel spectrogram tensors paired with padded token-ID sequences.
Evaluation outputs: per-sample error logs, WER/CER scores, confusion matrices, and code-switch metrics.
These structures facilitate efficient processing and detailed analysis.
System Models, Algorithms, and Interaction Flow
Architecture flow (textual):
Script Generator → TTS (JP/EN) → Noise Injector → Bilingual Dataset → Tokeniser → Fine-Tuned Whisper Model → Evaluation Module.
Interaction sequence:
User generates bilingual text → TTS audio → noise augmentation → fine-tuning → evaluation.
Core classes (textual UML):
Tokeniser (vocab, scripts; encode/decode)
AudioSample (transcript, labels; load/add_noise)
WhisperTrainer (model, loaders; training/validation)
Evaluator (predictions; WER/CER/switch accuracy)
Key algorithms include:
Tokenisation via BPE-style subword matching
SNR-controlled noise addition
Standard fine-tuning loop over Whisper’s encoder–decoder structure
These design elements collectively demonstrate clear methodological discipline.

Cross-Model Summary
Across the 15 displayed samples per model (CER reported for all categories), mean CER values are: Whisper Small 25.6% → 7.1% (baseline → fine-tuned); Whisper Medium 22.6% → 0.1%; Whisper Large-V3 24.5% → 0.2%. The progression from Small to Large-V3 also illustrates a shift in baseline failure mode on code-switching audio: from terse katakana transliteration (Small), to verbose katakana transliteration (Medium), to outright English-token deletion (Large-V3) — with fine-tuning resolving each in turn.
[Heading 6] Local Inference Benchmarks: Hardware Specifications and Runtime Performance
This appendix documents the hardware and software environment used for the local-inference demonstration of the three fine-tuned Whisper models, together with per-clip latency, peak GPU memory and Real-Time Factor (RTF) measurements. All measurements were taken on a single consumer-grade laptop on 17 May 2026. The same laptop was also used to record the accompanying video demonstration of the FastAPI web application. Google Colaboratory served only as the development environment for fine-tuning and the controlled re-measurement of peak-inference VRAM and was not used for the runtime benchmarks reported here. The aim of this appendix is to demonstrate that the fine-tuned models are practically deployable outside a dedicated server environment and to make the runtime characteristics transparent and reproducible. RTF is defined as inference time divided by audio duration; values below 1.0 indicate faster-than-real-time operation.
F.1  Hardware and Software Environment
The demonstration was performed on a Dell G15 5530 laptop running Microsoft Windows 11 Home (Build 10.0.26200). The CPU is a 13th-generation Intel Core i9-13900HX (Raptor Lake-HX, 24 physical cores, 32 logical processors, base clock 2.20 GHz). The system carries 15.7 GB of physical memory. GPU acceleration is provided by an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU (Ada Lovelace, CUDA compute capability 8.9) with 8 GB of GDDR6 memory (8,188 MiB total, of which 7,507 MiB was free at idle), a maximum SM clock of 3,105 MHz and a maximum memory clock of 8,001 MHz. The installed NVIDIA driver was version 591.74, reporting a maximum supported CUDA runtime version of 13.1.
Inference was run under Python 3.11.15 with PyTorch 2.12.0 compiled against CUDA 12.6 and Hugging Face Transformers 5.8.1. Project dependencies were managed with the uv package manager (version 0.11.14). Inference for Whisper Small and Whisper Medium used float16 precision; Whisper Large-V3 used bfloat16. F.2  Inference Benchmark Methodology
Six representative clips were used: two code-switching utterances (~17.3 s each), two Japanese-only utterances (~7.5–7.9 s) and two English-only utterances (~8.2–8.5 s). All clips were resampled to 16 kHz mono prior to feeding the Whisper feature extractor, in accordance with the model specification. Each model was loaded as a merged checkpoint (LoRA adapters merged into the base weights) from local disk and moved to the GPU before timing. One warm-up inference pass was executed per model before measurement, so that CUDA kernel compilation and lazy allocator overheads are excluded from the reported latencies. Peak GPU memory was captured per clip using torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated and includes both model weights and per-step activations. Per-clip inference time, RTF and peak GPU memory are reported in Table F2 (Whisper Small), Table F3 (Whisper Medium) and Table F4 (Whisper Large-V3).

Across all eighteen per-clip measurements the Real-Time Factor remains well below 1.0, meaning every model transcribes considerably faster than real time on the RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. The largest observed RTF, 0.099 for Whisper Medium on the 8.5-second English-only clip en_audio_003, still corresponds to roughly ten-times-real-time throughput. Combined peak-inference VRAM with all three merged models held simultaneously in memory is approximately 5.26 GB (0.54 + 1.59 + 3.13 GB), which fits comfortably within the 8 GB budget of the host GPU and leaves headroom for activations, the audio front-end and any concurrent processes. Two generation artefacts were observed during the demonstration runs and are reported here for transparency: a partial repetition on the code-switching clip code_switch_005 under Whisper Large-V3, and a Japanese-language hallucination on the English-only clip en_audio_003 under Whisper Medium. Both are well-documented Whisper decoding artefacts and can be mitigated in deployment by tightening beam-search parameters (for example, increasing no_repeat_ngram_size or forcing the appropriate language token). They do not reflect the corpus-level evaluation results reported in Chapter 5, which use the project's standard decoding configuration.
